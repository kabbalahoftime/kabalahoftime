# The daily companion — the summaries

One entry a day: a digestible summary of one KoT Book's writing for one week,
filed the way Binah files them. Day 1 is Book 1's post for week 1, day 2 is
Book 2's for week 1, day 8 is Book 1's for week 2, day 225 is Book 1's for
week 33. Seven books × fifty-two weeks = 364, and there are 364 posts, one to
each day.

**Every entry is a summary of its post, and of nothing else.** The posts are
on disk — `tools/fetch_blog.py` brings them down and
`tools/match_companion_posts.py` pairs each with its day — so every summary
can be checked against its source, which is what
`tools/check_companion_summaries.py` does. That is the whole difference
between this and the companion that came down in October: that one was
written from nothing and invented a motto for this app, a verse it does not
carry, and routes a reader could not follow.

Headings are `## Day N · Book B · Week W`. Paragraphs are the entry's body.
A day with no section here falls back to a plain transcription of the app's
own weekly table, so the companion is never short of an entry.

## Day 225 · Book 1 · Week 33

Week thirty-three is the week of Pesach Sheini and Lag Ba'Omer. Lag Ba'Omer is a day of great celebration, because it was then that the students of Rabbi Akiva stopped perishing. It is also the yahrzeit of Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai — Yesod ha'Olam, the foundation of the world — and the custom is to light a bonfire in his honor, representing the great light he brought to the world through his teachings of Kabbalah.

In Perek Shirah the creature of this week is the donkey, which proclaims: “Yours, O Lord, are the greatness, and the might, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty, for all that is in the heavens and on the earth is Yours; Yours is the kingdom and You are He Who is exalted over everything as the Leader.” (I Chronicles 29:11) King David recited this verse when he was at the height of his glory, reiterating that everything is from Hashem: glory and kingship, the Heavens and the earth.

In the Counting of the Omer this is the week of hod, acknowledgement — Hod shebeHod — so it is quite appropriate that the song of the donkey should so gracefully acknowledge that everything comes from G-d. The song contains all seven emotional sefirot: gedulah is a reference to chesed; then come gevurah, tiferet, netzach and hod; hakol is a reference to yesod; and mamlachah, a reference to malchut.

From Pirkei Avot, Rabbi Yochanan the son of Berokah: whoever desecrates the Divine Name covertly is punished in public, and regarding the desecration of the Name the malicious and the merely negligent are one and the same.

Pesach Sheini is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Meir.

*Source: [Week 33 (From the Book): To Recognize the Spiritual Treasures Hidden within Each One of Us](https://www.kabbalahoftime.com/2014/05/week-33-to-recognize-spiritual.html) — 1950 words, 2019-05-12*

## Day 226 · Book 2 · Week 33

The thirty-third week is the week of Pesach Sheini and Lag Ba'Omer, and in Haazinu the verse speaks again of the bitter punishment faced by the Jewish people, comparing it to wine: “Their wine is the bitterness of serpents, and the bitterness of the ruthless cobras.” (Deuteronomy 32:33) Read in a positive light, their deep Torah wisdom came from the bitter serpents, and their ability to be a head — to have a deep mind — from dealing with ruthless cobras.

Wine is also a known metaphor for the essence and the hidden part of the Torah, associated primarily with the works of Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai. The joy of Lag Ba'Omer comes in contrast to the suffering and the mourning of the Omer period, with the passing of twenty-four thousand of Rabbi Akiva's students. Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai himself acquired this wine through much suffering, and in the end he said that the suffering he endured was very much worth it.

The haftorah verse: “G-d is He who has fortified me with strength; and He looseth perfectly my path.” (II Samuel 22:33) The word for strength is chayil, a known reference to the Torah, the source of King David's power. The verse also carries a word written differently from how it is spoken — darchoh, His way, rather than darchi, my way — so that it reads: G-d makes clear to me His perfect way. The word for perfect is tamim, itself a reference to the Torah, as David says, Torat Hashem Tmimah Meshivat Nafesh.

The quality of this week is Ohev et HaBriot, loving the creations, the third term Rabbi Meir uses of those who study Torah lishmah. The prophet is Chabakuk; the Levitical city, Jarmuth.

## Day 227 · Book 3 · Week 33

Week thirty-three is the week of Pesach Sheini and Lag Ba'Omer, and the Torah portion section for this week first introduces the Mannah: “It came to pass in the evening that the quails went up and covered the camp, and in the morning there was a layer of dew around the camp. The layer of dew went up, and behold, on the surface of the desert, a fine, bare substance as fine as frost on the ground.”

The Mannah is fine as frost and compared to the dew, in contrast with the physicality of the quail. That contrast is also one between the revealed Torah, nigleh, and the Kabbalah, nistar. In nigleh there is back and forth and room for digestion — setting aside certain opinions and coming to conclusions. When it comes to Kabbalah there is no need for separating the good from the bad. There is also an opinion that the Mannah began falling on Lag Ba'Omer, and that this is the reason for its celebration.

The haftorah: “Blessed above women shall Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite, be; above women in the tent shall she be blessed.” The women in the tent are a reference to the blessings given to the matriarchs (Talmud, Sanhedrin 105). Similarly, Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai is said to be praiseworthy above all of his colleagues.

Daf Lamed Gimmel of Sotah continues the discussion of statements that must be said in Hebrew to be valid and those that may be said in any language, and the blessings and curses said at Mount Grizim and Mount Eival. It goes into the language of angels and heavenly voices, and contains a story of what Shimon HaTzadik heard in the Kodesh HaKodashim.

The generation is David. The journey: they journeyed from Ezion geber and camped in the desert of Zin, which is Kadesh.

## Day 1 · Book 1 · Week 1

Week one is the week of Rosh Hashanah, and the year's counting begins on the same weekday the Counting of the Omer begins — the second day of Passover. It takes in the days of selichot leading up to the holiday, when we rise early to ask forgiveness and start the year with a clean slate.

The first creature in Perek Shirah is the rooster, who says: "When the Holy One, blessed be He, comes to the righteous in the Garden of Eden, all the trees in the Garden of Eden scatter their spices, and they rejoice and praise, and then He, too, is aroused and praises." (Zohar, Vayakhel 195b) Then come seven calls, one to each day of the week. The first is "Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in! Who is this King of glory? G-d strong and mighty, G-d mighty in battle!" (Psalms 24:7-8) The second says it again, and answers differently: "G-d of hosts, He is the King of glory, Selah!" (Psalms 24:9-10) The fifth and sixth ask, "How long will you sleep, O sluggard? When will you arise from your sleep?" (Proverbs 6:9) and "Do not love sleep, lest you come to poverty." (Proverbs 20:13) The shofar is blown a hundred times and the rooster's verses contain a hundred words.

Pirkei Avot opens the same way: an introduction, then seven pairs of rabbis, and the lesson repeated through them is to acquire a master. On Rosh Hashanah we acquire the ultimate Master.

The pairing is Chesed shebeChesed, kindness within kindness. It was on Rosh Hashanah that Sarah, old and barren, was told she would bear Isaac, and that Chanah was told of Samuel. The month is Tishrei, whose tribe is Ephraim, who studied under his grandfather Jacob.

The rooster's own lesson is the plainest one: stop sleeping, get up, move forward. We thank G-d each morning for giving the rooster the understanding to tell day from night.

## Day 2 · Book 2 · Week 1

Haazinu's first verse opens the week: "Listen, O heavens, and I will speak! And let the earth hear the words of my mouth!" (Deuteronomy 32:1) Heavens and earth — which is how the Torah itself begins, and the week of Rosh Hashanah is the week of the world's creation.

The haftorah opens David's song and gives its reason: "on the day that the Lord delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul." (II Samuel 22:1) Saul — *Shaul* — is spelled exactly as *She'ol*, the pit, the grave. On Rosh Hashanah we too mark having lived to see another year, and hope to be inscribed in the Book of Life.

The first of the forty-eight ways of acquiring Torah is not in the list but in its introduction: "Torah is greater than priesthood and kingship, for kingship is acquired with 30 qualities, priesthood with 24, whereas the Torah is acquired with 48 ways." It points at Proverbs — the beginning of wisdom is to acquire wisdom. Rashi reads it as two steps: first learn from others and take the tradition from the mouth of a teacher, then with everything you have acquire understanding, working it out for yourself. That is Book 1's lesson again, from the other side.

The prophets are Avraham and Sarah, the first to go looking for knowledge of G-d. Sarah's birthday and her yahrzeit both fall on Rosh Hashanah, and so does the Akeidah.

The Levitical city is Shechem, also a city of refuge. *Shechem* means shoulder — the part the head sits on, and Rosh Hashanah is the head of the year. It is the first place Avraham, Yaakov and Yehoshua each came to on entering the Land: its gateway, as this week is the year's.

What the week asks for is *ratzon*, desire. Nothing stands in the way of desire, and Rebbe Nachman teaches that the wanting matters more than the outcome.

## Day 3 · Book 3 · Week 1

Book 3 opens where the Song of the Sea opens: "Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the Lord, and they spoke, saying, I will sing to the Lord, for very exalted is He." The haftorah answers it: "Now Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam sang on that day." Both verses are leaders of the people singing in unison, and Rosh Hashanah is about connecting to the head of the people.

The week's portion of Sotah is the passage the whole tractate rests on — the woman who is judged by water. If she was defiled the curse-bearing waters become bitter in her; if she was not, "she shall be exempted and bear seed." It is a judgment, which is what this week is.

The generation is Adam, the first man, who is the spiritual and physical source of all mankind. Rosh Hashanah is the day Adam was created, and it holds the same whole-of-humanity scope that he does.

The journey is the first one: "They journeyed from Rameses in the first month, on the fifteenth day of the first month; on the day following the Passover sacrifice, the children of Israel left triumphantly before the eyes of all the Egyptians. And the Egyptians were busy burying" — burying their firstborn, and occupied with their mourning. *Ramses* means the sun-god made him. The work of these days is to take in that we are Hashem's firstborn and that Hashem is the Creator; to leave Egypt, and to leave the idea that there could be any other god.

Adam's lesson for prayer is scale. Pray not only for yourself but for all mankind, and first for whoever needs it most. It is not by accident that every prayer we say is in the plural. We are more connected than we think.

## Day 4 · Book 4 · Week 1

The story of Chanah begins with her husband's lineage: "Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim-zophim, of the hill-country of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah, the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite." Tishrei's tribe is Ephraim, and Elkanah was the leader of his generation and a prophet in his own right. The verse opens with *Ish Echad*, one man — and this week everything is on the one. Ramathaim-Zophim means the heights that look into the future, which is what Rosh Hashanah does.

The sixth chapter of Pirkei Avot opens with the name of Rabbi Meir and with the subject the rest of the year will keep returning to: "Whoever studies Torah for Torah's sake alone, merits many things." Torah lishmah, learning for its own sake. Like Elkanah, Rabbi Meir stood head and shoulders above his generation. "Merits many things" reads as almost superfluous beside the spiritual gifts listed after it; some commentaries take it for the physical blessings. That is Rosh Hashanah exactly — a spiritual day on which this world's portions are settled.

Proverbs opens with the same subject in its own words: "To know wisdom and discipline, to comprehend words of understanding… The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge; fools despise wisdom and discipline."

The yahrzeit is 2 Tishrei, Rabbi Yisrael of Stolin, the Yenuka, who became rebbe at five years old — a rebbe higher than intellect, loved and feared by his chassidim. Like Samuel and Solomon, who also came to prominence as children, he lived to fifty-two. He is known as the Frankforter, because that is where he passed away; he asked to be buried in the place of his passing, and so lies near Rabbi Amnon of Mainz, who died on the first of Tishrei. Rabbi Amnon was maimed limb by limb for refusing to convert, asked to be carried to the Ark on Rosh Hashanah, and died as he finished saying *uNetaneh Tokef*.

## Day 5 · Book 5 · Week 1

"The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for your love is better than wine. Because of the fragrance of your goodly oils, your name is 'oil poured forth.' Therefore, the maidens loved you."

Song of Songs means the song that excels all songs, and perhaps the song that contains them all. Rosh Hashanah is the day that excels all days and contains them all. Every *Shlomoh* in Shir HaShirim is read as a reference to G-d: Solomon is the king, and this is the day we make Hashem King. Solomon's name also means peace is his, and on this day peace too is in Hashem's hands.

The second verse speaks of nearness, which is what the days of awe are. The third, the oils poured forth, is how Hashem's dealings with Israel give off a scent the whole world can smell — which is why the maidens, the nations, love Him as well. On Rosh Hashanah all mankind is judged, and the nations feel something of it. Wine and oil are the physical blessings of the day, and both are also figures for knowledge.

Of the seventy souls who went down to Egypt the first named is Reuven, the firstborn, whom Jacob calls "my first-born, my strength and the first of my vigor." Firstborn, from *rosh*, a head.

Shevuot is built from verses scattered through the Torah rather than one passage, and an oath, like this day and like this song, is an all-encompassing thing. Jeremiah's first chapter is all-encompassing too: it states the whole book's theme, the destruction coming to Israel and Jerusalem, and it holds the idea of a thing settled from the start — Jeremiah is told he was chosen while still in his mother's womb.

## Day 6 · Book 6 · Week 1

Book 6 is the week read in psalms, and for the week of Rosh Hashanah they are the first three, with the opening of the Tikkun HaKlali and the opening of Psalm 89.

Psalm 1 sets two ways against each other. "Fortunate is the man that has not walked in the counsel of the wicked, nor stood in the path of sinners, nor sat in the company of scoffers. Rather, his desire is in the Torah of the Lord, and in His Torah he meditates day and night. He shall be like a tree planted by streams of water, that yields its fruit in its season, and whose leaf does not wither." The wicked are chaff the wind drives away, and "the wicked will not endure in judgement."

Psalm 2 is the nations gathering and conspiring against the Lord and His anointed, and the answer from above: "He Who sits in heaven laughs." Then, plainly, "It is I Who have anointed My king, upon Zion, My holy mountain." It ends by telling the kings of the earth to serve with awe and rejoice with trembling.

Psalm 3 is David's, from when he fled from Absalom his son. Many rise against him and many say there is no salvation for him, and he answers: "But You, Lord, are a shield for me, my glory, the One Who raises my head." Then, "I lie down and sleep; I awake, for the Lord sustains me."

The Tikkun HaKlali's first psalm begins "Watch over me, O God, for I have put my trust in You," and Psalm 89 begins "I will sing of the Lord's kindness forever; to all generations I will make known Your faithfulness with my mouth."

Three psalms about judgment, kingship and being held up, in the week of the head of the year.

## Day 7 · Book 7 · Week 1

Book 7 gathers the week. Every verse the six books sang this week is laid into one song, line alternating with line, and the week's gathering is called after the first of them: the rooster is saying.

So the rooster's calls run through it with everything else threaded between them. "When the Holy One, blessed be He, comes to the righteous" meets "Listen, O heavens, and I will speak!" — "in the Garden of Eden, all the trees in the Garden of Eden" meets "And let the earth hear the words of my mouth!" Psalm 89's "To all generations I will make known Your faithfulness with my mouth" and "I will sing of the Lord's kindness forever" fall in beside the trees scattering their spices.

Then the gates. "Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O everlasting doors!" takes in David's song — "And David spoke to the Lord the words of this song" — and the opening of Song of Songs, and the Song of the Sea, "Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the Lord." Twice the question comes: "Who is this King of glory?" and twice the answer, G-d strong and mighty, G-d of hosts.

The rooster's remaining calls carry Elkanah's lineage and the Tikkun HaKlali with them. "Stand, O righteous ones, and busy yourselves with Torah" sits beside "of the hill-country of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah"; "How long will you sleep, O sluggard?" beside "Those who hasten after other gods, their sorrows shall increase"; and the last call, "It is time to act for G-d; for they have made void Your Torah," beside "the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite."

Six readings, one voice. It closes on the Song of the Sea: "I will sing to the Lord, for very exalted is He."

## Day 8 · Book 1 · Week 2

Week two is the week of Yom Kippur, and the creature is the hen: "He gives bread to all flesh, for His kindness endures forever." (Psalms 136:25) She sings of G-d feeding every living thing, in the days when it is settled how much each will receive — and who will live and who will not.

Two customs of this week run through the hen. Eating well on the eve of Yom Kippur is held to be as meritorious as the fast itself. And it is in this week that Kapparot is done, the ancient custom of atoning through a hen, which is then slaughtered and given to families in need; many now use charity money instead.

Pirkei Avot gives Shimon, the son of Rabban Gamliel: "All my life I have been raised among the wise, and I have found nothing better for the body than silence. The essential thing is not study, but deed. And one who speaks excessively brings on sin." To take in what another says you first have to be quiet. And the silencing of the body is this day plainly — the fast, no leather shoes, no anointing, no relations. For one day we stand apart from the physical, like angels.

Week one was about acquiring one teacher. This week it is the wise in the plural. One is unity and two is multiplicity, and they do not contradict: a person can have one master and still learn from everyone.

The pairing is Gevurah shebeChesed, discipline inside kindness — which is the fast itself, self-control exercised while begging for mercy from a G-d we know to be just and kind.

The day also insists that deeds are what count. Its high point is the reading of Jonah, which turns on "And G-d saw their actions."

After the rooster got us out of bed, the hen's lesson is the next step: eat properly, take care of the body you were given.

## Day 9 · Book 2 · Week 2

Haazinu turns to water: "My lesson will drip like rain; my word will flow like dew; like storm winds on vegetation and like raindrops on grass." (Deuteronomy 32:2) Rain and dew — the beginning of time again, and the Flood. Water is purity and it is life, which are not two things. Dew in particular belongs to resurrection: it was with dew that G-d revived the Jewish people at the giving of the Torah, and the Torah itself is called dew.

The haftorah answers with shelter: "The Lord is my rock and my fortress, and a rescuer to me." (II Samuel 22:2) On Yom Kippur we are the ones delivered.

The week's quality is constant study. Last week was desire; this week is discipline, because Torah is not acquired without it. The prophet is Isaac, who is discipline — *gevurah* — and whose dedication is read off the Akeidah. One opinion in the Talmud puts the Akeidah on Yom Kippur. Like Jews on this day, Isaac lived a heavenly life while still on earth; the Zohar says he spent three years in the Garden of Eden afterwards. He is called a pure offering, and he never once left the Land of Israel.

The Levitical city is Gezer, a place so strategic that it is among the most excavated sites in Israel, and a wedding gift from Pharaoh when his daughter married Solomon. *Gezer* shares its root with *gzerah*, a decree — and the business of this day is annulling evil decrees through prayer, repentance and charity.

What constant study asks for in practice is a fixed time. It is one of the questions put to a person in the World to Come: did you set times for Torah? The Alter Rebbe adds that the times must be fixed not only in the day but in the soul.

## Day 10 · Book 3 · Week 2

The Song of the Sea reaches the drowning: "a horse and its rider He cast into the sea. The Eternal's strength and His vengeance were my salvation." That is Yom Kippur — we throw ourselves into the water and come out clean. Rabbi Akiva says it outright: Hashem Himself is our mikvah. He is our strength and our song and our salvation, and it is on this day that we actually know it.

The haftorah speaks of "when breaches are made in Israel, when the people offer themselves willingly, bless the Lord." Breaches, *phra'ot*, has the same root as Pharaoh — who stands for the oversized ego and for stubbornness. Seeing what the ego costs, and giving ourselves over willingly, is how the blessing gets said.

The tractate opens here. Daf Beit, the first daf of Sotah, is about how couples are matched and brought together — souls paired before they come down. Two is the number of a mate.

The generation is Seth, who was given to Adam and Eve as consolation for Abel. That belongs to this week too, which is spent undoing what was done, or better, turning it into merit. *Shet* means foundation and source — like the Even Shetiah, the Foundation Stone the world was made from, which sat in the Holy of Holies, the room entered once a year, on this day.

The journey is the second: "The children of Israel journeyed from Rameses and camped in Succoth." Last week was taking in that Hashem is Father and Creator; this week it is Hashem as protector — and in the calendar, after Yom Kippur, we go straight out to build the sukkah.

Seth's lesson for prayer is direction. He is the source, and prayer runs the other way: bringing blessing down, the spiritual into the material.

## Day 11 · Book 4 · Week 2

"And he had two wives; the name of the one was Hannah and the name of the second was Peninnah; and Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children." Two wives — two, and marriage, and relationship, which is this week. Peninah means pearl, the beauty that is inside. Channah comes from *chen*, grace and also mercy; Rebbe Nachman makes chen essential to prayer, and the Talmud learns how to pray from Channah herself. All of it is this day: through prayer and fasting the inner beauty is uncovered, grace is found, the past is washed.

Pirkei Avot says of the one who studies Torah for its own sake that "not only that, but the creation of the entire world is worthwhile for him alone." Set that beside the verse: Peninah had children and Channah had none, and yet Peninah and all her children were worth creating because of Channah. The Talmud says the same of Rabbi Chanina — whose name is also from chen — "The whole world is nourished because of Chanina, and for Chanina, one amount of carob is enough from Sabbath eve to the next." (Brachot 17b)

Proverbs' second chapter lays out the two roads: "for her house sinks to death, and her paths lead to the dead… in order that you go in the way of the good, and you keep the ways of the righteous. For the upright shall dwell in the land."

Two yahrzeits. On the eighth of Tishrei, Rabbi Noach of Lechovitch, a link in the chain to Slonim, Kobrin, Koidenov and Karlin, who taught that a Jew who begins to doubt his faith should have faith that he has faith — that it is there, only hidden. On the ninth, the Magen Avraham, among the greatest authorities in Jewish law, sickly as a child, who wrote that when someone wrongs you, do not hold the grudge in silence: say "Why did you do this to me?" — in private, calmly, with soft language.

## Day 12 · Book 5 · Week 2

"Draw me, we will run after you; the king brought me to his chambers." Having been given a glimpse of being drawn near, we run. And on this one day of the year the Kohen Gadol goes into the inner chamber, the Holy of Holies.

The Talmud calls Yom Kippur, with Tu B'Av, the happiest day of the year — which is a startling thing to say of a day with no feasting and no wine. Rashi reads the wine of this verse as standing for every physical festivity.

Then the song turns to what we are: "I am black but comely, O daughters of Jerusalem! Like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon." We have sinned and we are beautiful underneath it, and teshuvah is still open. Rashi: black as the tents of Kedar, blackened by rain because they stand out in the desert — and as easily cleaned, to be like the curtains of Solomon. The next verse is the admission: "they made me a keeper of the vineyards; my own vineyard I did not keep." We were to be a light to the nations and could not keep our own.

Of the seventy souls the second is Enoch, Reuven's firstborn — who carries the name of that earlier Enoch, so righteous that Hashem took him alive and he became an angel. Which is what this day asks of us.

Shevuot opens with laws that "are two that are really four," the doubling of a second week, and its first daf gives most of itself to the Yom Kippur offerings.

Jeremiah's second chapter is the verse Rashi was quoting: "I remember to you the lovingkindness of your youth, the love of your nuptials, your following Me in the desert, in a land not sown." Shavuot was the wedding. This day is the second one, when the second tablets were given.

## Day 13 · Book 6 · Week 2

The week of Yom Kippur in psalms is the fourth, fifth and sixth, with three verses of the Tikkun HaKlali and one of Psalm 89.

Psalm 4 is a man asking to be heard and telling others to stop: "Answer me when I call, O God Who knows my righteousness. You have relieved me in my distress." Then, "Tremble and do not sin; reflect in your hearts upon your beds, and be silent forever." Against those who ask who will show us good, he answers that the joy put in his heart is greater than theirs when grain and wine abound, and ends, "In peace and harmony I will lie down and sleep, for You, Lord, will make me dwell alone, in security."

Psalm 5 is prayer at first light. "Lord, hear my voice in the morning; in the morning I set my prayers before you and hope." It is blunt about what cannot stand before G-d — the boastful, the speakers of falsehood, whose throat is an open grave though their tongue flatters — and it closes on shelter: "You will envelop him with favor as with a shield."

Psalm 6 is the one said from the floor. "Lord, do not punish me in Your anger… heal me, O Lord, for my bones tremble in fear. My soul is panic-stricken; and You, O Lord, how long?" Then the line that gives the reason: "For there is no remembrance of You in death; who will praise You in the grave?" And: "I am weary from sighing; each night I drench my bed, I melt my couch with my tears." It turns at the end — "The Lord has heard my supplication; the Lord accepts my prayer."

The Tikkun HaKlali's verses are contentment with what was given: "The Lord is my allotted portion and my cup; You guide my destiny. Portions have fallen to me in pleasant places." Psalm 89 holds the week's foundation: "Forever will it be built with kindness."

## Day 14 · Book 7 · Week 2

The hen opens the gathering, and the week's six readings come in behind her, a line at a time.

"He gives bread to all flesh, for His kindness endures forever" meets the haftorah's shelter — "The Lord is my rock and my fortress, and a rescuer to me" — and then the running: "Draw me, we will run after you." The willing offering follows, "when the people offer themselves willingly, bless the Lord."

Haazinu's water is broken in two and set on either side of the rejoicing. "My lesson will drip like rain" / "We will rejoice and be glad in you" / "My word will flow like dew" / "They have loved you sincerely" — and then Psalm 89's building, "as the heavens, with which You will establish Your faithfulness."

The sea comes next, "a horse and its rider He cast into the sea," answered by the tents of Kedar and the curtains of Solomon, and by "You guide my destiny." Then the king's chambers, and immediately the two wives: "And he had two wives; the name of the one was Hannah."

From there the song braids the vineyard with the barrenness. "They made me a keeper of the vineyards" / "even the inheritance pleases me" / "my own vineyard I did not keep" / "and the name of the second was Peninnah; and Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children." The mother's sons who were incensed sit beside the breaches made in Israel, and "I am black but comely" beside "portions have fallen to me in pleasant places."

It ends where a day like this one would want to end: "Even at night my conscience instructs me. I will bless the Lord, Who counseled me."

## Day 15 · Book 1 · Week 3

Week three is Sukkot, and the dove sings: "Like a swift or crane, so do I chatter; I moan like a dove, my eyes fail with looking upward; O G-d, I am oppressed, be my security." (Isaiah 38:14) She says one thing more, before the Holy One: "Master of the World! May my sustenance be as bitter as an olive in Your hands, rather than it being sweet as honey through flesh and blood." (Talmud, Eruvin 18b)

That is the whole week in a sentence. We dip the challah in honey and we eat under branches. The word the dove uses for security, *arveni*, means be my guarantor — and can also be read be sweet to me. It is the word behind *Kol Israel Arevim Zeh LaZeh*, that every Jew is responsible for, and mixed up with, and sweet to every other, which is what Sukkot is about.

The sukkah is a flimsy thing, open to the weather. We do our part and we know the rest is not ours. The dove is loyalty: the Torah likens Israel to a dove and tefillin to its wings, and the wings guard the dove as the mitzvot guard us. She is also the bearer of good news — it was the dove Noah sent, who came back with an olive branch.

Three runs through everything here. Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Shimon the son of Gamliel: "By three things is the world sustained: law, truth and peace." (Zachariah 8:16) Those are the three holidays we have now passed: Rosh Hashanah is the Day of Din, judgment; on Yom Kippur we are sealed, and G-d's seal is truth; and Sukkot is peace, the One who spreads a sukkah of peace over us. The world stands on three pillars — Torah, Divine service, acts of kindness — and so on the three fathers, with Jacob third, who is *tiferet*, the balance between Abraham's kindness and Isaac's strength. After parting from Esau, Jacob went to a place called Sukkot.

The pairing is Tiferet shebeChesed. This week the yahrzeit of the Rebbe Maharash falls, who was born on the seventeenth day of the Omer — tiferet within tiferet — and whose father sometimes called him by it.

And this week we are commanded to be happy. The dove's lesson is not to be anxious, but also to make a vessel: to be grateful, and to keep the place you live in as ordered and pleasant as a sukkah.

## Day 16 · Book 2 · Week 3

"When I call out the name of the Lord, ascribe greatness to our G-d." (Deuteronomy 32:3) The day after Yom Kippur is called G-d's name, and the four days between Yom Kippur and Sukkot answer to the four letters of it. Then comes the ascribing of greatness, which on Sukkot is done with the hands: a good sukkah, a beautiful etrog, a beautiful lulav. That is what the sages read in "This is my G-d and I will glorify Him." (Exodus 15:2; Shabbat 133b)

The haftorah piles up the shelter: "G-d is my rock, under whom I take cover; My shield, and the horn of my salvation, my support, and my refuge; He is my savior Who saves me from violence." (II Samuel 22:3) Last week's protection was large and far off. This one is close enough to sit under, which is the sukkah.

The quality is attentive listening — *shmi'at haOzen*, the listening of the ear. The ear is where the body keeps its balance, and it is the organ of this holiday. Listening is also what it is to be a vessel: you cannot receive a teaching while talking. On Sukkot we are all vessels, and the blessing comes down through the roof.

The prophet is Jacob, who is this holiday twice over — he travelled to a place called Sukkot, and his yahrzeit falls in it. He is listening itself: a plain man who dwelt in tents, and the Shema opens "Listen, O Israel," where the Midrash reads Israel as Jacob himself.

The Levitical city is Kibzaim, which means two gatherings. Sukkot is Chag Ha'Asif, the festival of the ingathering, when the harvest comes in — and its offerings come in two kinds, those we bring for ourselves and those we bring for the other nations.

What constant study taught last week, listening tempers this week: be realistic. Enthusiasm sets a schedule nobody can keep. Add a little at a time, as a lifter adds weight. *Tafastah merubah, lo tafastah* — grab too much and you have grabbed nothing.

## Day 17 · Book 3 · Week 3

"This is my G-d, and I will make Him a habitation, the G-d of my father, and I will ascribe to Him exaltation." A house built to honour Him — which is the sukkah, and which is also *Zeh Keili v'Anvehu*, the beautiful etrog and the beautiful lulav.

The haftorah is all ears: "Hear, O kings, give ear, O princes; I, to the Lord I shall sing." *Anochi* comes twice over, and Rashi reads it as the great revelation that happens under the sukkah — like receiving the Torah over again. Our sins have been let go, and we are happy enough to sing.

Sotah's third daf is about how a Divine spirit enters a person, and Sukkot was the season when people received Ruach HaKodesh.

The generation is Enosh, whose name also means mankind as a whole, and on Sukkot we pray on behalf of the whole world.

The journey is out of the shelter: "They journeyed from Succoth and camped in Etham, at the edge of the desert." Etham can mean a fortress — not unlike a sukkah — and in ancient Egyptian, a seashore. The work of this week is the edge: to take in everything the shelter is giving while you are still in it, so that you can do something with it when you walk out into the world. A small child is entirely inside the fortress. Then comes the shoreline, where you go a little further out and run back for approval, not yet in the desert but at its edge, meeting a world that is not interested in you.

Enosh's lesson is about knowing where you are weak. The Hayom Yom gives four words for a person: *Adam* is mind, *Ish* is heart, *Enosh* is weakness in one or the other or both, and *Gever* is the one who gets past it — Gever works on Enosh to raise him to Ish or Adam. Which means Enosh already has what they have, or it could not be raised. As Rebbe Nachman said: if you believe you can break, believe you can fix.

## Day 18 · Book 4 · Week 3

"And this man went up out of his city from year to year to worship and to sacrifice unto the Lord of hosts in Shiloh. And the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were there priests unto the Lord." Three priests in one verse, and Elkanah going up year after year — Sukkot being one of the three festivals you go up for.

Rashi notices the tense: he *would* go up, season after season. And the Midrash adds the detail that makes him a tzadik rather than merely observant — the road he took one year he did not take the next, so that different people would see him going, and come too. He went up, and the number of pilgrims went up with him.

Pirkei Avot this week says of the one who learns Torah for its own sake that he is called a friend. Elkanah is what that looks like. And this is the week of walking into each other's sukkot.

Proverbs' third chapter is the same subject in instructions: "Do not withhold good from the one who needs it when you have power in your hand to do it. Do not say to your fellow, 'Go and return, and tomorrow I will give,' though you have it with you. Devise no harm against your fellow, when he dwells securely with you. Do not quarrel with anyone without cause, if he did you no harm."

On the fourteenth of Tishrei is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Yisrael Hopstein, the Maggid of Kozhnitz, whom his chassidim called the second Baal Shem Tov. He was born to elderly parents after a blessing from the Baal Shem Tov — and the blessing was earned by their joy, by his father the bookbinder breaking into a dance at the Shabbat table. The Baal Shem Tov smiled that Friday night because he could see the heavenly court dancing along.

He was sickly as a child and a prodigy. As a young man he studied Magen Avraham with a friend before dawn, and came away so alight that he went to ask Rabbi Shmelke of Nickelsburg where the light had come from. Before he could ask, Rabbi Shmelke said: Israel, I can see on your face that you studied Magen Avraham. That book generates a great light in the heart of the wise.

The next day, the first day of Sukkot, is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Mordechai of Lechovitch, whose teaching turns on happiness.

## Day 19 · Book 5 · Week 3

"Tell me, you whom my soul loves, where do you feed, where do you rest the flocks at noon, for why should I be like one who veils herself beside the flocks of your companions?"

Rashi makes the question harder and better: where do You feed Your flock among these wolves they are standing in the middle of, and where do You rest them at noon — in this exile, which is as hard on them as noon is on a flock. In the sukkah we are out in the weather and we feel the noon sun. The companions are the other nations, and on Sukkot we bring offerings for them too.

Then G-d answers, and because we have been judged and have turned back, He calls us the fairest of women: "go your way in the footsteps of the flocks and pasture your kids beside the shepherds' dwellings." The shepherds' dwellings may be the seven shepherds who come into the sukkah.

The third verse goes back further — Pharaoh's chariots and their horses, silenced. All that was asked of us then was to keep still and trust, which is what the sukkah asks now, remembering forty years of being kept.

Of the seventy souls the third is Pallu, whose name is close to *peleh*, a wonder: the clouds of glory, the manna, the forty years of them.

Shevuot's third daf takes up oaths about eating, and the law of carrying in and out of a home — which is eating in the sukkah, and making the sukkah a home. It also treats lashes for things not done, and the sukkah is the mitzvah you keep by sitting still inside it.

Jeremiah's third chapter answers the Song: "Return, backsliding children, says the Lord, for I possessed you… And I will give you shepherds according to My heart, and they will feed you with knowledge and understanding." And later, on where to put us: "How shall I place you among the sons? But I will give you a desirable land… Call Me 'my Father,' and do not turn away from following Me." All the nations are called sons there, and we bring for all of them.

## Day 20 · Book 6 · Week 3

The first part of Sukkot in psalms is the seventh, eighth and ninth, with three verses of the Tikkun HaKlali and one of Psalm 89.

Psalm 7 is a man under pursuit who submits himself to examination. "I put my trust in You, Lord, my G-d; deliver me from all my pursuers and save me. Lest he tear my soul like a lion, crushing me with none to rescue." Then the oath: if I have done this, if there is wrongdoing in my hands, if I have rewarded my friends with evil — then let the enemy overtake me. It settles on the judge: "G-d is the righteous judge," the "Searcher of hearts and minds." And it watches the trap work backwards: "He digs a pit, digs it deep, only to fall into the trap he laid. His mischief will return upon his own head."

Psalm 8 steps outside and looks up. "Lord, our Master, how mighty is Your Name throughout the earth, You Who has set Your majesty upon the heavens! Out of the mouths of babes and sucklings You have established might." Then the question the night sky puts: "When I behold Your heavens, the work of Your fingers, the moon and the stars which You have set in place — what is man that You should remember him?" And the answer, which does not shrink him: "Yet, You have made him but a little less than the angels, and crowned him with honor and glory."

Psalm 9 is thanks and the throne. "I will thank the Lord with all my heart; I will recount all Your wonders… You sat on the throne, O righteous Judge." Its mercy is specific: "The Lord will be a stronghold for the oppressed, a stronghold in times of trouble… He does not forget the cry of the downtrodden."

The Tikkun HaKlali's verses are steadiness: "I have set the Lord before me at all times; because He is at my right hand, I shall not falter. Therefore my heart rejoices and my soul exults; my flesh, too, rests secure." And Psalm 89 swears: "I have made a covenant with My chosen one; I have sworn to David, My servant."

## Day 21 · Book 7 · Week 3

The dove opens, and this week the gathering is full of listening and sheltering.

"Like a swift or crane, so do I chatter" is answered by "Hear, O kings, give ear, O princes," and then Haazinu — "When I call out the name of the Lord" / "Ascribe greatness to our G-d" — and the Song of the Sea's own ascribing: "the G-d of my father, and I will ascribe to Him exaltation."

Then the dove speaks before the Holy One, and the haftorah's rescue falls in beside her, with Elkanah going up: "He is my savior Who saves me from violence" / "to worship and to sacrifice unto Hashem of hosts in Shiloh."

The moaning and the cover come together. "I moan like a dove, my eyes fail with looking upward" / "G-d is my rock, under whom I take cover" / "This is my G-d, and I will make Him a habitation" / "I have set the Lord before me at all times" / "O G-d, I am oppressed, be my security."

The olive and the honey sit at the centre — "May my sustenance be as bitter as an olive in Your hands, rather than it being sweet as honey through flesh and blood" — with "My support, and my refuge" and "Because He is at my right hand, I shall not falter" on either side of "Master of the World!"

Toward the end the covenant and the flocks braid together: "I have made a covenant with My chosen one" / "if you do not know, O fairest of women" / "go your way in the footsteps of the flocks" / "my flesh, too, rests secure" / "I have sworn to David, My servant." Hophni and Phinehas come in at Shiloh, and the song ends where the shepherds are: "where do you feed, where do you rest the flocks at noon" — "and pasture your kids beside the shepherds' dwellings."
