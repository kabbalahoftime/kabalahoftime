# The daily companion — the summaries

One entry a day: a digestible summary of one KoT Book's writing for one week,
filed the way Binah files them. Day 1 is Book 1's post for week 1, day 2 is
Book 2's for week 1, day 8 is Book 1's for week 2, day 225 is Book 1's for
week 33. Seven books × fifty-two weeks = 364, and there are 364 posts, one to
each day.

**Every entry is a summary of its post, and of nothing else.** The posts are
on disk — `tools/fetch_blog.py` brings them down and
`tools/match_companion_posts.py` pairs each with its day — so every summary
can be checked against its source, which is what
`tools/check_companion_summaries.py` does. That is the whole difference
between this and the companion that came down in October: that one was
written from nothing and invented a motto for this app, a verse it does not
carry, and routes a reader could not follow.

Headings are `## Day N · Book B · Week W`. Paragraphs are the entry's body.
A day with no section here falls back to a plain transcription of the app's
own weekly table, so the companion is never short of an entry.

## Day 225 · Book 1 · Week 33

Week thirty-three is the week of Pesach Sheini and Lag Ba'Omer. Lag Ba'Omer is a day of great celebration, because it was then that the students of Rabbi Akiva stopped perishing. It is also the yahrzeit of Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai — Yesod ha'Olam, the foundation of the world — and the custom is to light a bonfire in his honor, representing the great light he brought to the world through his teachings of Kabbalah.

In Perek Shirah the creature of this week is the donkey, which proclaims: “Yours, O Lord, are the greatness, and the might, and the glory, and the victory, and the majesty, for all that is in the heavens and on the earth is Yours; Yours is the kingdom and You are He Who is exalted over everything as the Leader.” (I Chronicles 29:11) King David recited this verse when he was at the height of his glory, reiterating that everything is from Hashem: glory and kingship, the Heavens and the earth.

In the Counting of the Omer this is the week of hod, acknowledgement — Hod shebeHod — so it is quite appropriate that the song of the donkey should so gracefully acknowledge that everything comes from G-d. The song contains all seven emotional sefirot: gedulah is a reference to chesed; then come gevurah, tiferet, netzach and hod; hakol is a reference to yesod; and mamlachah, a reference to malchut.

From Pirkei Avot, Rabbi Yochanan the son of Berokah: whoever desecrates the Divine Name covertly is punished in public, and regarding the desecration of the Name the malicious and the merely negligent are one and the same.

Pesach Sheini is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Meir.

## Day 226 · Book 2 · Week 33

The thirty-third week is the week of Pesach Sheini and Lag Ba'Omer, and in Haazinu the verse speaks again of the bitter punishment faced by the Jewish people, comparing it to wine: “Their wine is the bitterness of serpents, and the bitterness of the ruthless cobras.” (Deuteronomy 32:33) Read in a positive light, their deep Torah wisdom came from the bitter serpents, and their ability to be a head — to have a deep mind — from dealing with ruthless cobras.

Wine is also a known metaphor for the essence and the hidden part of the Torah, associated primarily with the works of Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai. The joy of Lag Ba'Omer comes in contrast to the suffering and the mourning of the Omer period, with the passing of twenty-four thousand of Rabbi Akiva's students. Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai himself acquired this wine through much suffering, and in the end he said that the suffering he endured was very much worth it.

The haftorah verse: “G-d is He who has fortified me with strength; and He looseth perfectly my path.” (II Samuel 22:33) The word for strength is chayil, a known reference to the Torah, the source of King David's power. The verse also carries a word written differently from how it is spoken — darchoh, His way, rather than darchi, my way — so that it reads: G-d makes clear to me His perfect way. The word for perfect is tamim, itself a reference to the Torah, as David says, Torat Hashem Tmimah Meshivat Nafesh.

The quality of this week is Ohev et HaBriot, loving the creations, the third term Rabbi Meir uses of those who study Torah lishmah. The prophet is Chabakuk; the Levitical city, Jarmuth.

## Day 227 · Book 3 · Week 33

Week thirty-three is the week of Pesach Sheini and Lag Ba'Omer, and the Torah portion section for this week first introduces the Mannah: “It came to pass in the evening that the quails went up and covered the camp, and in the morning there was a layer of dew around the camp. The layer of dew went up, and behold, on the surface of the desert, a fine, bare substance as fine as frost on the ground.”

The Mannah is fine as frost and compared to the dew, in contrast with the physicality of the quail. That contrast is also one between the revealed Torah, nigleh, and the Kabbalah, nistar. In nigleh there is back and forth and room for digestion — setting aside certain opinions and coming to conclusions. When it comes to Kabbalah there is no need for separating the good from the bad. There is also an opinion that the Mannah began falling on Lag Ba'Omer, and that this is the reason for its celebration.

The haftorah: “Blessed above women shall Jael, the wife of Heber the Kenite, be; above women in the tent shall she be blessed.” The women in the tent are a reference to the blessings given to the matriarchs (Talmud, Sanhedrin 105). Similarly, Rabbi Shimon Bar Yochai is said to be praiseworthy above all of his colleagues.

Daf Lamed Gimmel of Sotah continues the discussion of statements that must be said in Hebrew to be valid and those that may be said in any language, and the blessings and curses said at Mount Grizim and Mount Eival. It goes into the language of angels and heavenly voices, and contains a story of what Shimon HaTzadik heard in the Kodesh HaKodashim.

The generation is David. The journey: they journeyed from Ezion geber and camped in the desert of Zin, which is Kadesh.

## Day 1 · Book 1 · Week 1

Week one is the week of Rosh Hashanah, and the year's counting begins on the same weekday the Counting of the Omer begins — the second day of Passover. It takes in the days of selichot leading up to the holiday, when we rise early to ask forgiveness and start the year with a clean slate.

The first creature in Perek Shirah is the rooster, who says: "When the Holy One, blessed be He, comes to the righteous in the Garden of Eden, all the trees in the Garden of Eden scatter their spices, and they rejoice and praise, and then He, too, is aroused and praises." (Zohar, Vayakhel 195b) Then come seven calls, one to each day of the week. The first is "Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O everlasting doors! And the King of glory shall come in! Who is this King of glory? G-d strong and mighty, G-d mighty in battle!" (Psalms 24:7-8) The second says it again, and answers differently: "G-d of hosts, He is the King of glory, Selah!" (Psalms 24:9-10) The fifth and sixth ask, "How long will you sleep, O sluggard? When will you arise from your sleep?" (Proverbs 6:9) and "Do not love sleep, lest you come to poverty." (Proverbs 20:13) The shofar is blown a hundred times and the rooster's verses contain a hundred words.

Pirkei Avot opens the same way: an introduction, then seven pairs of rabbis, and the lesson repeated through them is to acquire a master. On Rosh Hashanah we acquire the ultimate Master.

The pairing is Chesed shebeChesed, kindness within kindness. It was on Rosh Hashanah that Sarah, old and barren, was told she would bear Isaac, and that Chanah was told of Samuel. The month is Tishrei, whose tribe is Ephraim, who studied under his grandfather Jacob.

The rooster's own lesson is the plainest one: stop sleeping, get up, move forward. We thank G-d each morning for giving the rooster the understanding to tell day from night.

## Day 2 · Book 2 · Week 1

Haazinu's first verse opens the week: "Listen, O heavens, and I will speak! And let the earth hear the words of my mouth!" (Deuteronomy 32:1) Heavens and earth — which is how the Torah itself begins, and the week of Rosh Hashanah is the week of the world's creation.

The haftorah opens David's song and gives its reason: "on the day that the Lord delivered him from the hand of all his enemies, and from the hand of Saul." (II Samuel 22:1) Saul — *Shaul* — is spelled exactly as *She'ol*, the pit, the grave. On Rosh Hashanah we too mark having lived to see another year, and hope to be inscribed in the Book of Life.

The first of the forty-eight ways of acquiring Torah is not in the list but in its introduction: "Torah is greater than priesthood and kingship, for kingship is acquired with 30 qualities, priesthood with 24, whereas the Torah is acquired with 48 ways." It points at Proverbs — the beginning of wisdom is to acquire wisdom. Rashi reads it as two steps: first learn from others and take the tradition from the mouth of a teacher, then with everything you have acquire understanding, working it out for yourself. That is Book 1's lesson again, from the other side.

The prophets are Avraham and Sarah, the first to go looking for knowledge of G-d. Sarah's birthday and her yahrzeit both fall on Rosh Hashanah, and so does the Akeidah.

The Levitical city is Shechem, also a city of refuge. *Shechem* means shoulder — the part the head sits on, and Rosh Hashanah is the head of the year. It is the first place Avraham, Yaakov and Yehoshua each came to on entering the Land: its gateway, as this week is the year's.

What the week asks for is *ratzon*, desire. Nothing stands in the way of desire, and Rebbe Nachman teaches that the wanting matters more than the outcome.

## Day 3 · Book 3 · Week 1

Book 3 opens where the Song of the Sea opens: "Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the Lord, and they spoke, saying, I will sing to the Lord, for very exalted is He." The haftorah answers it: "Now Deborah and Barak the son of Abinoam sang on that day." Both verses are leaders of the people singing in unison, and Rosh Hashanah is about connecting to the head of the people.

The week's portion of Sotah is the passage the whole tractate rests on — the woman who is judged by water. If she was defiled the curse-bearing waters become bitter in her; if she was not, "she shall be exempted and bear seed." It is a judgment, which is what this week is.

The generation is Adam, the first man, who is the spiritual and physical source of all mankind. Rosh Hashanah is the day Adam was created, and it holds the same whole-of-humanity scope that he does.

The journey is the first one: "They journeyed from Rameses in the first month, on the fifteenth day of the first month; on the day following the Passover sacrifice, the children of Israel left triumphantly before the eyes of all the Egyptians. And the Egyptians were busy burying" — burying their firstborn, and occupied with their mourning. *Ramses* means the sun-god made him. The work of these days is to take in that we are Hashem's firstborn and that Hashem is the Creator; to leave Egypt, and to leave the idea that there could be any other god.

Adam's lesson for prayer is scale. Pray not only for yourself but for all mankind, and first for whoever needs it most. It is not by accident that every prayer we say is in the plural. We are more connected than we think.

## Day 4 · Book 4 · Week 1

The story of Chanah begins with her husband's lineage: "Now there was a certain man of Ramathaim-zophim, of the hill-country of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah, the son of Jeroham, the son of Elihu, the son of Tohu, the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite." Tishrei's tribe is Ephraim, and Elkanah was the leader of his generation and a prophet in his own right. The verse opens with *Ish Echad*, one man — and this week everything is on the one. Ramathaim-Zophim means the heights that look into the future, which is what Rosh Hashanah does.

The sixth chapter of Pirkei Avot opens with the name of Rabbi Meir and with the subject the rest of the year will keep returning to: "Whoever studies Torah for Torah's sake alone, merits many things." Torah lishmah, learning for its own sake. Like Elkanah, Rabbi Meir stood head and shoulders above his generation. "Merits many things" reads as almost superfluous beside the spiritual gifts listed after it; some commentaries take it for the physical blessings. That is Rosh Hashanah exactly — a spiritual day on which this world's portions are settled.

Proverbs opens with the same subject in its own words: "To know wisdom and discipline, to comprehend words of understanding… The fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge; fools despise wisdom and discipline."

The yahrzeit is 2 Tishrei, Rabbi Yisrael of Stolin, the Yenuka, who became rebbe at five years old — a rebbe higher than intellect, loved and feared by his chassidim. Like Samuel and Solomon, who also came to prominence as children, he lived to fifty-two. He is known as the Frankforter, because that is where he passed away; he asked to be buried in the place of his passing, and so lies near Rabbi Amnon of Mainz, who died on the first of Tishrei. Rabbi Amnon was maimed limb by limb for refusing to convert, asked to be carried to the Ark on Rosh Hashanah, and died as he finished saying *uNetaneh Tokef*.

## Day 5 · Book 5 · Week 1

"The Song of Songs, which is Solomon's. Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth, for your love is better than wine. Because of the fragrance of your goodly oils, your name is 'oil poured forth.' Therefore, the maidens loved you."

Song of Songs means the song that excels all songs, and perhaps the song that contains them all. Rosh Hashanah is the day that excels all days and contains them all. Every *Shlomoh* in Shir HaShirim is read as a reference to G-d: Solomon is the king, and this is the day we make Hashem King. Solomon's name also means peace is his, and on this day peace too is in Hashem's hands.

The second verse speaks of nearness, which is what the days of awe are. The third, the oils poured forth, is how Hashem's dealings with Israel give off a scent the whole world can smell — which is why the maidens, the nations, love Him as well. On Rosh Hashanah all mankind is judged, and the nations feel something of it. Wine and oil are the physical blessings of the day, and both are also figures for knowledge.

Of the seventy souls who went down to Egypt the first named is Reuven, the firstborn, whom Jacob calls "my first-born, my strength and the first of my vigor." Firstborn, from *rosh*, a head.

Shevuot is built from verses scattered through the Torah rather than one passage, and an oath, like this day and like this song, is an all-encompassing thing. Jeremiah's first chapter is all-encompassing too: it states the whole book's theme, the destruction coming to Israel and Jerusalem, and it holds the idea of a thing settled from the start — Jeremiah is told he was chosen while still in his mother's womb.

## Day 6 · Book 6 · Week 1

Book 6 is the week read in psalms, and for the week of Rosh Hashanah they are the first three, with the opening of the Tikkun HaKlali and the opening of Psalm 89.

Psalm 1 sets two ways against each other. "Fortunate is the man that has not walked in the counsel of the wicked, nor stood in the path of sinners, nor sat in the company of scoffers. Rather, his desire is in the Torah of the Lord, and in His Torah he meditates day and night. He shall be like a tree planted by streams of water, that yields its fruit in its season, and whose leaf does not wither." The wicked are chaff the wind drives away, and "the wicked will not endure in judgement."

Psalm 2 is the nations gathering and conspiring against the Lord and His anointed, and the answer from above: "He Who sits in heaven laughs." Then, plainly, "It is I Who have anointed My king, upon Zion, My holy mountain." It ends by telling the kings of the earth to serve with awe and rejoice with trembling.

Psalm 3 is David's, from when he fled from Absalom his son. Many rise against him and many say there is no salvation for him, and he answers: "But You, Lord, are a shield for me, my glory, the One Who raises my head." Then, "I lie down and sleep; I awake, for the Lord sustains me."

The Tikkun HaKlali's first psalm begins "Watch over me, O God, for I have put my trust in You," and Psalm 89 begins "I will sing of the Lord's kindness forever; to all generations I will make known Your faithfulness with my mouth."

Three psalms about judgment, kingship and being held up, in the week of the head of the year.

## Day 7 · Book 7 · Week 1

Book 7 gathers the week. Every verse the six books sang this week is laid into one song, line alternating with line, and the week's gathering is called after the first of them: the rooster is saying.

So the rooster's calls run through it with everything else threaded between them. "When the Holy One, blessed be He, comes to the righteous" meets "Listen, O heavens, and I will speak!" — "in the Garden of Eden, all the trees in the Garden of Eden" meets "And let the earth hear the words of my mouth!" Psalm 89's "To all generations I will make known Your faithfulness with my mouth" and "I will sing of the Lord's kindness forever" fall in beside the trees scattering their spices.

Then the gates. "Lift up your heads, O gates! And be lifted up, O everlasting doors!" takes in David's song — "And David spoke to the Lord the words of this song" — and the opening of Song of Songs, and the Song of the Sea, "Then Moses and the children of Israel sang this song to the Lord." Twice the question comes: "Who is this King of glory?" and twice the answer, G-d strong and mighty, G-d of hosts.

The rooster's remaining calls carry Elkanah's lineage and the Tikkun HaKlali with them. "Stand, O righteous ones, and busy yourselves with Torah" sits beside "of the hill-country of Ephraim, and his name was Elkanah"; "How long will you sleep, O sluggard?" beside "Those who hasten after other gods, their sorrows shall increase"; and the last call, "It is time to act for G-d; for they have made void Your Torah," beside "the son of Zuph, an Ephraimite."

Six readings, one voice. It closes on the Song of the Sea: "I will sing to the Lord, for very exalted is He."

## Day 8 · Book 1 · Week 2

Week two is the week of Yom Kippur, and the creature is the hen: "He gives bread to all flesh, for His kindness endures forever." (Psalms 136:25) She sings of G-d feeding every living thing, in the days when it is settled how much each will receive — and who will live and who will not.

Two customs of this week run through the hen. Eating well on the eve of Yom Kippur is held to be as meritorious as the fast itself. And it is in this week that Kapparot is done, the ancient custom of atoning through a hen, which is then slaughtered and given to families in need; many now use charity money instead.

Pirkei Avot gives Shimon, the son of Rabban Gamliel: "All my life I have been raised among the wise, and I have found nothing better for the body than silence. The essential thing is not study, but deed. And one who speaks excessively brings on sin." To take in what another says you first have to be quiet. And the silencing of the body is this day plainly — the fast, no leather shoes, no anointing, no relations. For one day we stand apart from the physical, like angels.

Week one was about acquiring one teacher. This week it is the wise in the plural. One is unity and two is multiplicity, and they do not contradict: a person can have one master and still learn from everyone.

The pairing is Gevurah shebeChesed, discipline inside kindness — which is the fast itself, self-control exercised while begging for mercy from a G-d we know to be just and kind.

The day also insists that deeds are what count. Its high point is the reading of Jonah, which turns on "And G-d saw their actions."

After the rooster got us out of bed, the hen's lesson is the next step: eat properly, take care of the body you were given.

## Day 9 · Book 2 · Week 2

Haazinu turns to water: "My lesson will drip like rain; my word will flow like dew; like storm winds on vegetation and like raindrops on grass." (Deuteronomy 32:2) Rain and dew — the beginning of time again, and the Flood. Water is purity and it is life, which are not two things. Dew in particular belongs to resurrection: it was with dew that G-d revived the Jewish people at the giving of the Torah, and the Torah itself is called dew.

The haftorah answers with shelter: "The Lord is my rock and my fortress, and a rescuer to me." (II Samuel 22:2) On Yom Kippur we are the ones delivered.

The week's quality is constant study. Last week was desire; this week is discipline, because Torah is not acquired without it. The prophet is Isaac, who is discipline — *gevurah* — and whose dedication is read off the Akeidah. One opinion in the Talmud puts the Akeidah on Yom Kippur. Like Jews on this day, Isaac lived a heavenly life while still on earth; the Zohar says he spent three years in the Garden of Eden afterwards. He is called a pure offering, and he never once left the Land of Israel.

The Levitical city is Gezer, a place so strategic that it is among the most excavated sites in Israel, and a wedding gift from Pharaoh when his daughter married Solomon. *Gezer* shares its root with *gzerah*, a decree — and the business of this day is annulling evil decrees through prayer, repentance and charity.

What constant study asks for in practice is a fixed time. It is one of the questions put to a person in the World to Come: did you set times for Torah? The Alter Rebbe adds that the times must be fixed not only in the day but in the soul.

## Day 10 · Book 3 · Week 2

The Song of the Sea reaches the drowning: "a horse and its rider He cast into the sea. The Eternal's strength and His vengeance were my salvation." That is Yom Kippur — we throw ourselves into the water and come out clean. Rabbi Akiva says it outright: Hashem Himself is our mikvah. He is our strength and our song and our salvation, and it is on this day that we actually know it.

The haftorah speaks of "when breaches are made in Israel, when the people offer themselves willingly, bless the Lord." Breaches, *phra'ot*, has the same root as Pharaoh — who stands for the oversized ego and for stubbornness. Seeing what the ego costs, and giving ourselves over willingly, is how the blessing gets said.

The tractate opens here. Daf Beit, the first daf of Sotah, is about how couples are matched and brought together — souls paired before they come down. Two is the number of a mate.

The generation is Seth, who was given to Adam and Eve as consolation for Abel. That belongs to this week too, which is spent undoing what was done, or better, turning it into merit. *Shet* means foundation and source — like the Even Shetiah, the Foundation Stone the world was made from, which sat in the Holy of Holies, the room entered once a year, on this day.

The journey is the second: "The children of Israel journeyed from Rameses and camped in Succoth." Last week was taking in that Hashem is Father and Creator; this week it is Hashem as protector — and in the calendar, after Yom Kippur, we go straight out to build the sukkah.

Seth's lesson for prayer is direction. He is the source, and prayer runs the other way: bringing blessing down, the spiritual into the material.

## Day 11 · Book 4 · Week 2

"And he had two wives; the name of the one was Hannah and the name of the second was Peninnah; and Peninnah had children, but Hannah had no children." Two wives — two, and marriage, and relationship, which is this week. Peninah means pearl, the beauty that is inside. Channah comes from *chen*, grace and also mercy; Rebbe Nachman makes chen essential to prayer, and the Talmud learns how to pray from Channah herself. All of it is this day: through prayer and fasting the inner beauty is uncovered, grace is found, the past is washed.

Pirkei Avot says of the one who studies Torah for its own sake that "not only that, but the creation of the entire world is worthwhile for him alone." Set that beside the verse: Peninah had children and Channah had none, and yet Peninah and all her children were worth creating because of Channah. The Talmud says the same of Rabbi Chanina — whose name is also from chen — "The whole world is nourished because of Chanina, and for Chanina, one amount of carob is enough from Sabbath eve to the next." (Brachot 17b)

Proverbs' second chapter lays out the two roads: "for her house sinks to death, and her paths lead to the dead… in order that you go in the way of the good, and you keep the ways of the righteous. For the upright shall dwell in the land."

Two yahrzeits. On the eighth of Tishrei, Rabbi Noach of Lechovitch, a link in the chain to Slonim, Kobrin, Koidenov and Karlin, who taught that a Jew who begins to doubt his faith should have faith that he has faith — that it is there, only hidden. On the ninth, the Magen Avraham, among the greatest authorities in Jewish law, sickly as a child, who wrote that when someone wrongs you, do not hold the grudge in silence: say "Why did you do this to me?" — in private, calmly, with soft language.

## Day 12 · Book 5 · Week 2

"Draw me, we will run after you; the king brought me to his chambers." Having been given a glimpse of being drawn near, we run. And on this one day of the year the Kohen Gadol goes into the inner chamber, the Holy of Holies.

The Talmud calls Yom Kippur, with Tu B'Av, the happiest day of the year — which is a startling thing to say of a day with no feasting and no wine. Rashi reads the wine of this verse as standing for every physical festivity.

Then the song turns to what we are: "I am black but comely, O daughters of Jerusalem! Like the tents of Kedar, like the curtains of Solomon." We have sinned and we are beautiful underneath it, and teshuvah is still open. Rashi: black as the tents of Kedar, blackened by rain because they stand out in the desert — and as easily cleaned, to be like the curtains of Solomon. The next verse is the admission: "they made me a keeper of the vineyards; my own vineyard I did not keep." We were to be a light to the nations and could not keep our own.

Of the seventy souls the second is Enoch, Reuven's firstborn — who carries the name of that earlier Enoch, so righteous that Hashem took him alive and he became an angel. Which is what this day asks of us.

Shevuot opens with laws that "are two that are really four," the doubling of a second week, and its first daf gives most of itself to the Yom Kippur offerings.

Jeremiah's second chapter is the verse Rashi was quoting: "I remember to you the lovingkindness of your youth, the love of your nuptials, your following Me in the desert, in a land not sown." Shavuot was the wedding. This day is the second one, when the second tablets were given.

## Day 13 · Book 6 · Week 2

The week of Yom Kippur in psalms is the fourth, fifth and sixth, with three verses of the Tikkun HaKlali and one of Psalm 89.

## Day 15 · Book 1 · Week 3

Week three is Sukkot, and the dove sings: "Like a swift or crane, so do I chatter; I moan like a dove, my eyes fail with looking upward; O G-d, I am oppressed, be my security." (Isaiah 38:14) She says one thing more, before the Holy One: "Master of the World! May my sustenance be as bitter as an olive in Your hands, rather than it being sweet as honey through flesh and blood." (Talmud, Eruvin 18b)

That is the whole week in a sentence. We dip the challah in honey and we eat under branches. The word the dove uses for security, *arveni*, means be my guarantor — and can also be read be sweet to me. It is the word behind *Kol Israel Arevim Zeh LaZeh*, that every Jew is responsible for, and mixed up with, and sweet to every other, which is what Sukkot is about.

The sukkah is a flimsy thing, open to the weather. We do our part and we know the rest is not ours. The dove is loyalty: the Torah likens Israel to a dove and tefillin to its wings, and the wings guard the dove as the mitzvot guard us. She is also the bearer of good news — it was the dove Noah sent, who came back with an olive branch.

Three runs through everything here. Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Shimon the son of Gamliel: "By three things is the world sustained: law, truth and peace." (Zachariah 8:16) Those are the three holidays we have now passed: Rosh Hashanah is the Day of Din, judgment; on Yom Kippur we are sealed, and G-d's seal is truth; and Sukkot is peace, the One who spreads a sukkah of peace over us. The world stands on three pillars — Torah, Divine service, acts of kindness — and so on the three fathers, with Jacob third, who is *tiferet*, the balance between Abraham's kindness and Isaac's strength. After parting from Esau, Jacob went to a place called Sukkot.

The pairing is Tiferet shebeChesed. This week the yahrzeit of the Rebbe Maharash falls, who was born on the seventeenth day of the Omer — tiferet within tiferet — and whose father sometimes called him by it.

And this week we are commanded to be happy. The dove's lesson is not to be anxious, but also to make a vessel: to be grateful, and to keep the place you live in as ordered and pleasant as a sukkah.

## Day 16 · Book 2 · Week 3

"When I call out the name of the Lord, ascribe greatness to our G-d." (Deuteronomy 32:3) The day after Yom Kippur is called G-d's name, and the four days between Yom Kippur and Sukkot answer to the four letters of it. Then comes the ascribing of greatness, which on Sukkot is done with the hands: a good sukkah, a beautiful etrog, a beautiful lulav. That is what the sages read in "This is my G-d and I will glorify Him." (Exodus 15:2; Shabbat 133b)

The haftorah piles up the shelter: "G-d is my rock, under whom I take cover; My shield, and the horn of my salvation, my support, and my refuge; He is my savior Who saves me from violence." (II Samuel 22:3) Last week's protection was large and far off. This one is close enough to sit under, which is the sukkah.

The quality is attentive listening — *shmi'at haOzen*, the listening of the ear. The ear is where the body keeps its balance, and it is the organ of this holiday. Listening is also what it is to be a vessel: you cannot receive a teaching while talking. On Sukkot we are all vessels, and the blessing comes down through the roof.

The prophet is Jacob, who is this holiday twice over — he travelled to a place called Sukkot, and his yahrzeit falls in it. He is listening itself: a plain man who dwelt in tents, and the Shema opens "Listen, O Israel," where the Midrash reads Israel as Jacob himself.

The Levitical city is Kibzaim, which means two gatherings. Sukkot is Chag Ha'Asif, the festival of the ingathering, when the harvest comes in — and its offerings come in two kinds, those we bring for ourselves and those we bring for the other nations.

What constant study taught last week, listening tempers this week: be realistic. Enthusiasm sets a schedule nobody can keep. Add a little at a time, as a lifter adds weight. *Tafastah merubah, lo tafastah* — grab too much and you have grabbed nothing.

## Day 17 · Book 3 · Week 3

"This is my G-d, and I will make Him a habitation, the G-d of my father, and I will ascribe to Him exaltation." A house built to honour Him — which is the sukkah, and which is also *Zeh Keili v'Anvehu*, the beautiful etrog and the beautiful lulav.

The haftorah is all ears: "Hear, O kings, give ear, O princes; I, to the Lord I shall sing." *Anochi* comes twice over, and Rashi reads it as the great revelation that happens under the sukkah — like receiving the Torah over again. Our sins have been let go, and we are happy enough to sing.

Sotah's third daf is about how a Divine spirit enters a person, and Sukkot was the season when people received Ruach HaKodesh.

The generation is Enosh, whose name also means mankind as a whole, and on Sukkot we pray on behalf of the whole world.

The journey is out of the shelter: "They journeyed from Succoth and camped in Etham, at the edge of the desert." Etham can mean a fortress — not unlike a sukkah — and in ancient Egyptian, a seashore. The work of this week is the edge: to take in everything the shelter is giving while you are still in it, so that you can do something with it when you walk out into the world. A small child is entirely inside the fortress. Then comes the shoreline, where you go a little further out and run back for approval, not yet in the desert but at its edge, meeting a world that is not interested in you.

Enosh's lesson is about knowing where you are weak. The Hayom Yom gives four words for a person: *Adam* is mind, *Ish* is heart, *Enosh* is weakness in one or the other or both, and *Gever* is the one who gets past it — Gever works on Enosh to raise him to Ish or Adam. Which means Enosh already has what they have, or it could not be raised. As Rebbe Nachman said: if you believe you can break, believe you can fix.

## Day 18 · Book 4 · Week 3

"And this man went up out of his city from year to year to worship and to sacrifice unto the Lord of hosts in Shiloh. And the two sons of Eli, Hophni and Phinehas, were there priests unto the Lord." Three priests in one verse, and Elkanah going up year after year — Sukkot being one of the three festivals you go up for.

Rashi notices the tense: he *would* go up, season after season. And the Midrash adds the detail that makes him a tzadik rather than merely observant — the road he took one year he did not take the next, so that different people would see him going, and come too. He went up, and the number of pilgrims went up with him.

Pirkei Avot this week says of the one who learns Torah for its own sake that he is called a friend. Elkanah is what that looks like. And this is the week of walking into each other's sukkot.

Proverbs' third chapter is the same subject in instructions: "Do not withhold good from the one who needs it when you have power in your hand to do it. Do not say to your fellow, 'Go and return, and tomorrow I will give,' though you have it with you. Devise no harm against your fellow, when he dwells securely with you. Do not quarrel with anyone without cause, if he did you no harm."

On the fourteenth of Tishrei is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Yisrael Hopstein, the Maggid of Kozhnitz, whom his chassidim called the second Baal Shem Tov. He was born to elderly parents after a blessing from the Baal Shem Tov — and the blessing was earned by their joy, by his father the bookbinder breaking into a dance at the Shabbat table. The Baal Shem Tov smiled that Friday night because he could see the heavenly court dancing along.

He was sickly as a child and a prodigy. As a young man he studied Magen Avraham with a friend before dawn, and came away so alight that he went to ask Rabbi Shmelke of Nickelsburg where the light had come from. Before he could ask, Rabbi Shmelke said: Israel, I can see on your face that you studied Magen Avraham. That book generates a great light in the heart of the wise.

The next day, the first day of Sukkot, is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Mordechai of Lechovitch, whose teaching turns on happiness.

## Day 19 · Book 5 · Week 3

"Tell me, you whom my soul loves, where do you feed, where do you rest the flocks at noon, for why should I be like one who veils herself beside the flocks of your companions?"

Rashi makes the question harder and better: where do You feed Your flock among these wolves they are standing in the middle of, and where do You rest them at noon — in this exile, which is as hard on them as noon is on a flock. In the sukkah we are out in the weather and we feel the noon sun. The companions are the other nations, and on Sukkot we bring offerings for them too.

Then G-d answers, and because we have been judged and have turned back, He calls us the fairest of women: "go your way in the footsteps of the flocks and pasture your kids beside the shepherds' dwellings." The shepherds' dwellings may be the seven shepherds who come into the sukkah.

The third verse goes back further — Pharaoh's chariots and their horses, silenced. All that was asked of us then was to keep still and trust, which is what the sukkah asks now, remembering forty years of being kept.

Of the seventy souls the third is Pallu, whose name is close to *peleh*, a wonder: the clouds of glory, the manna, the forty years of them.

Shevuot's third daf takes up oaths about eating, and the law of carrying in and out of a home — which is eating in the sukkah, and making the sukkah a home. It also treats lashes for things not done, and the sukkah is the mitzvah you keep by sitting still inside it.

Jeremiah's third chapter answers the Song: "Return, backsliding children, says the Lord, for I possessed you… And I will give you shepherds according to My heart, and they will feed you with knowledge and understanding." And later, on where to put us: "How shall I place you among the sons? But I will give you a desirable land… Call Me 'my Father,' and do not turn away from following Me." All the nations are called sons there, and we bring for all of them.

## Day 22 · Book 1 · Week 4

Week four holds the end of Sukkot — Hoshanah Rabbah, Shemini Atzeret, Simchat Torah — and the eagle sings: "And You, G-d, Lord of Hosts, Lord of Israel, awake to remember all the nations; do not be gracious to any wicked traitors, sela!" (Psalms 59:6) To remember can mean for good or for punishment, and the second half of her verse leans to the second.

That is the turn this week makes. All through Sukkot we have been bringing offerings for the nations. On Shemini Atzeret they stop, and the Jewish people are alone with G-d.

The eagle is the highest-flying bird, which gives her the widest view of everything made. Unlike other birds she carries her young on her wings rather than in her talons, because nothing can reach them up there: "You have seen what I did unto the Egyptians, and how I took them on eagles' wings and brought them to Me." (Exodus 19:4) She renews her feathers year after year, and in this week each community finishes the whole Torah and starts it again at once. And for the hoshanot and the hakafot we spend these days circling the bimah, which is what an eagle does.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Yehudah HaNassi: be as careful with a small mitzvah as with a great one, because you do not know what each is worth. Some people suppose that dancing with the Torah counts for less than the prayers of Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur. It does not.

He then names three things to keep in mind, which are really four: know what is above you — a seeing eye, a listening ear, and all your deeds written in a book. Those are the four holidays we have just walked through. Rosh Hashanah is knowing; on Yom Kippur He sees; Sukkot is the ear; and on Hoshanah Rabbah the judgment is finished and written.

The pairing is Netzach shebeChesed. The Torah ends with Moses blessing each of the twelve tribes, and Moses is netzach — victory and endurance, which is what finishing feels like. Rebbe Nachman, whose yahrzeit falls in this week, said of himself that his sefirah was netzach: "I have been victorious and I will be victorious; I have finished and I will finish." Two of his great themes are to be always happy and to always begin again, which is Simchat Torah exactly.

The eagle's lesson is to care about more than yourself. Caring for others is also one of the better ways out of sadness.

## Day 23 · Book 2 · Week 4

"The deeds of the Mighty Rock are perfect, for all His ways are justice; a faithful God, without injustice He is righteous and upright." (Deuteronomy 32:4) On Hoshanah Rabbah the year's judgment is sent out, and this is the verse for a judgment you can trust.

The haftorah answers with noise: "With praise, I call to the Lord, for from my enemies I shall be saved." (II Samuel 22:4) These are the days of calling out and dancing — and it echoes the eagle, who asked G-d to remember the nations and not to favour traitors.

The quality is verbal enunciation, saying the thing out loud. Hoshanah Rabbah is built on it: all the hoshanot of the previous days are said aloud, and the blessings of the coming year are brought down by speech. Then on Shemini Atzeret and Simchat Torah the Torah is taken up in a way that is not thought at all. We lift it with our bodies and dance. Torah cannot stay in the head; it has to come out through the mouth and into the world.

The prophet is Moses, who is this quality's hardest case. Speech did not come easily to him — in large part because his soul was so high that bringing its thoughts down into words was difficult. Which is the work of these days: getting what is pure in thought down into action, and into the dancing.

The Levitical city is Beth-Horon, a hollow house. On Shemini Atzeret we say goodbye to the sukkah and leave it empty — and carry its holiness out with us, into the year and into the world.

What this quality asks in practice is that you talk about what you learn. If Torah is only in study hours it is not yet real; it has to get into ordinary conversation, and be told to someone else. Proverbs says, a mitzvah is a candle and the Torah is light. A candle stands in one place. Light travels.

## Day 24 · Book 3 · Week 4

"The Lord is a Master of war; the Lord is His Name. Pharaoh's chariots and his army He cast into the sea." The haftorah stands in the same place: "I shall sing to the Lord, the G-d of Israel. Lord, when You went forth out of Seir" — Seir being where Esau lives. Three books this week are about the enemy being dealt with.

Sotah's daf takes up *yichud*, being shut in alone with someone. That is what Shemini Atzeret is: the day G-d keeps for Himself with the Jewish people, after the guests have gone.

The generation is Keinan, whose name looks like a version of Kayin — Cain — with an extra *nun*, the letter that stands elsewhere for the fiftieth gate, the power of prayer and of teshuvah. His whole line runs parallel to Cain's, as a kosher version of it, and humanity carries on through Keinan while Cain's line is set aside. Though not entirely: Noah's wife Na'amah came from Cain's side.

The journey goes to the hard place: "They journeyed from Etham and camped in Pi hahiroth, which faces Baal zephon; and they camped in front of Migdol." Last week was the edge of the desert. This week is the mouth of the rocks, camped in front of a place of idolatry. The work is to lift the material world and bring all of it to worship the one G-d.

Keinan's lesson is that serving G-d is a *kinyan*, an acquisition — the root inside both Keinan and Cain. To acquire a thing you have to give something of yourself, usually what you worked for. The last days of Sukkot are a marathon: intense, and tiring, and what you come out with is measured by what you put in. That is why it is called *avodah*, which means work.

## Day 25 · Book 4 · Week 4

"And it came to pass upon a day, when Elkanah sacrificed, that he gave to Peninnah his wife, and to all her sons and her daughters, portions." Portions for everyone — which is Sukkot, when offerings are brought for all the nations. And the verse is a set-up for the next one, where he gives Hannah a double portion. Which is what happens now: after the week of everyone, a day is kept for the Jewish people alone.

Shemini Atzeret is the hour after a great party, when the guests have gone home and only the one He loves is still there. And the word Pirkei Avot gives this week is exactly that: *beloved*.

Proverbs' fourth chapter is a chapter about not letting go. "Do not leave her, and she will preserve you; love her and she will guard you. ## Day 26 · Book 5 · Week 4

"Your cheeks are comely with rows, your neck with necklaces. We will make you rows of gold with studs of silver." These two are G-d speaking to us, and Rashi reads them as the spoils of the sea, when Israel emptied Egypt out — physically, and of its sparks.

The words themselves sound like this week. *Torim*, rows, are also lines: lines of dancers, or lines of a song. *Charuzim*, studs, are also rhymes — the piyyutim of the hakafot and of Tefilat Geshem. And silver, Chassidus says, comes from *kissufim*, longing: the wanting to hold on to G-d in these days. Rashi adds a forehead plate to the rows. He adorns us and we adorn Him.

The strongest hint is in the first word. *Lehi* is a cheek, and also a jawbone — the jawbone of a donkey, *chamor*, matter, with which Samson struck down a thousand Philistines, and out of which water came. The great dancing of Sukkot is the water drawing, and out of that water came Ruach HaKodesh, as the spirit rested on Samson in that fight.

The Baal Shem Tov, on Simchat Torah: Michael boasted that the archangel Metat ties crowns for G-d out of Israel's prayers — "Today, I shall fashion an even more glorious crown for the Almighty out of these torn shoes."

Then the third verse: "While the king was still at his table, my spikenard gave forth its fragrance." Sukkot belongs to all the nations, and then on the last day He keeps a private audience for us alone — just ourselves, at His table. (Mishnah, Sukkah 5:2)

Its later verses keep returning to the voice of the shofar, as the season of judgment closes.

## Day 29 · Book 1 · Week 5

Week five is Rosh Chodesh Cheshvan, and the crane asks for instruments: "Give thanks to G-d with the lyre; make music for Him with the ten-stringed harp." (Psalms 33:2) Not the voice alone. With a lyre, the music made for Him is more beautiful than it was.

That is the month. Cheshvan belongs to the tribe of Menashe, Joseph's firstborn, who helped his father run the whole Egyptian empire. Tishrei was for gathering holiness; Cheshvan is for carrying it into ordinary work and spending it there, using every power and tool and technology we have.

Pirkei Avot says the same thing in Rabban Gamliel the son of Rabbi Yehudah HaNassi: "Beautiful is the study of Torah with the way of the world, for the toil of them both causes sin to be forgotten. Ultimately, all Torah study that is not accompanied with work is destined to cease and to cause sin." He goes on about serving the community for the sake of Heaven, and about governments, who "befriend a person only for their own needs… they do not stand by a person at the time of his distress."

Then Hillel, on living among people: do not separate yourself from the community; do not trust yourself until the day you die; do not judge your fellow until you have stood in his place. And do not say "when I free myself of my concerns, I will study" — because you may never be free. In a place where there are no men, strive to be a man.

Hillel also saw a skull floating on the water and said to it: because you drowned others, you were drowned; and those who drowned you will themselves be drowned. A fitting thing at the opening of Cheshvan, which is the month of the Flood — and those waters stand for the material concerns that threaten to drown a person.

The pairing is Hod shebeChesed, which belongs to Aaron and to the service of the Kohanim. Cheshvan is the month in which the third Temple is to be inaugurated, and that is where the Kohanim will lift the material world through what they offer.

The crane's own lesson is music. It is one of the oldest ways of fighting sadness: David played the harp to lift Saul out of his torment, and the Levites sang while the Kohanim worked.

## Day 30 · Book 2 · Week 5

"Destruction is not His; it is His children's defect you crooked and twisted generation." (Deuteronomy 32:5) Cheshvan is the month of the Mabul, the Flood in the time of Noah, and this is a verse about a generation that brought its own ruin.

The haftorah is nearer still to that water: "For the pains of death have encompassed me; streams of scoundrels would affright me." (II Samuel 22:5) *Nachalei Bli'al*, the streams of scoundrels, read more literally as rivers of G-dlessness.

The quality is perception of the heart, *Binat Ha'Lev*. Noah's great mistake was not using his heart to see that G-d wanted him to pray for the rest of mankind. Prayer is called the service of the heart — and *teivah*, the word for ark, also means a word, which is prayer. It is prayer that keeps a person above the water. The Torah also calls the makers of the Temple's vessels *Chochmei Lev*, wise of heart.

The prophet is Aharon, whom perception of the heart describes better than anything else. He is the mediator, the one who could make peace between people and between a husband and a wife. And he was the first Kohen Gadol, serving in the Mishkan, the Temple that travelled.

The Levitical city is Taanach, whose origin is unclear but looks to come from *lehi'taanot*, to fast. There is a custom among the pious to fast on the first Monday, Thursday and Monday of Cheshvan — *BaHaB*. Not fasting for its own sake: to mark the line between the holidays of Tishrei and what comes after, and to get some hold over the physical. Which is the month's whole work, conquering the world with what Tishrei gave.

And the quality asks one more thing: let the Torah you learn reach your heart, not only your mind. As one of my Talmud teachers would ask — do you feel the question? If you do not feel the question you cannot really value the answer. And if the Torah is not in your heart you will not reach anyone else's.

## Day 31 · Book 3 · Week 5

"And the elite of his officers sank in the Red Sea. The depths covered them; they descended into the depths like a stone." The haftorah adds, "when You marched out of the field of Edom, the earth trembled, the heavens also dripped." Officers going under, depths closing over, heavens dripping — the Flood is in all of it, when the whole world went under its own depths.

The field matters too. Jacob was the wholesome man who sat in tents; his brother Eisav was the man of the field, out in the affairs of the world. Ephraim, who belongs to Tishrei, studied with Jacob as a child. Menashe's place is the field — ground that once belonged to Eisav alone.

Sotah's fifth daf takes up haughtiness and adultery, which are the two things that brought the Flood on. The Torah names sexual corruption and violent robbery — *chamas* — as its causes: "And the land was corrupted and the land was filled with violent robbery." Once the boundaries of personal conduct go, everything becomes permitted. And Noah himself lacked a kind of humility, praying for his own household and not for anyone else; Avraham and Moshe did not do that.

The generation is Mahalalel, who looks like a kosher version of Mehijael, of the line of Cain. Mahalalel holds *halel*, praise; Mehijael is spelled with a *chet* where Mahalalel has a *hay*. A hay is a chet with an opening in it. Chet belongs to sin; hay belongs to malchut and to teshuvah, and the opening is the door the returning person comes back through — teshuvah read as *tashuv hey*, restoring the hay.

The journey leaves the water: "They journeyed from Penei hahiroth and crossed in the midst of the sea to the desert. They walked for three days in the desert of Etham and camped in Marah." Note the change from *pi*, a mouth, to *pnei*, a face. Three days without water, and then bitter water — Marah, which is why Cheshvan is called Mar Cheshvan, bitter, having no holidays yet. The last stage of growing up is leaving the sheltered world of water for the dry one. Moses had to push them out of the sea into the desert; they did not want to go. And at Marah, when the water could not be drunk, he threw in a tree and it became drinkable. Bitterness comes to everyone, and there are two things to do with it: be overwhelmed, or find the sweetness buried in it, which is also where healing comes from.

Mahalalel's lesson for prayer is in his name: *Hallel veHoda'ah*, praise and thanks. Prayer is serving with the whole heart — getting excited, singing, dancing if you can. Read off a page by routine, there is a good chance nothing is being done at all. You have to know before Whom you are standing, and be glad about it.

## Day 32 · Book 4 · Week 5

"But unto Hannah he gave a double portion; for he loved Hannah, but the Lord had shut up her womb." Loved, and childless. It is Rachel's story again — Jacob loved her and she had no children — and one of the few dates Cheshvan does mark is Rachel's yahrzeit, on the eleventh.

The week's quality is *Ohev et HaMakom*, loves G-d, which reads literally as loving the Place. G-d is called the Place because He is not in the world; the world is in Him, and there is nowhere without Him. Which is the point just now: stepping out of the Garden of Eden that is Tishrei into the danger of Cheshvan and the world's business, He is still there. We are still inside Him.

Proverbs' fifth chapter is about love and about what can go wrong once you are out in the world: "For the lips of a strange woman drip honey, and her palate is smoother than oil. But her end is as bitter as wormwood, as sharp as a two-edged sword." And then the other way: "Drink water from your own cistern and running water from your own spring… you shall always be intoxicated with her love." The yahrzeits stay with Rizhin. On the twenty-ninth of Tishrei, Rav Menachem Mendel of Vizhnitz, the Rizhiner's son-in-law and the founder of Vizhnitz; and often this week the Rizhiner himself, on the third of Cheshvan.

The Tzemach Tzaddik said that he had just understood something that had bothered him all his life — why on Friday night we welcome the angels and then so soon send them off. Because angels cannot taste food, and we do not want to eat in front of them. Then he put down his own fork, which said who he thought he was sitting with.

Also usually this week, Rabbi Yisrael Spira, the Bluzhover Rebbe, who survived the camps and lost his wife and children there. He lit Chanukah candles in Bergen-Belsen, and was asked how he could possibly say *Sheheheyanu* — who has kept us alive and brought us to this time — while thousands were dying. That G-d has Jews like that is reason enough to say it.

## Day 33 · Book 5 · Week 5

## Day 36 · Book 1 · Week 6

Week six is the second week of Cheshvan, and the songbird sings about a house: "The songbird has also found her home, and the sparrow a nest for herself, where she may lay her young — Your altars, G-d of Hosts – my King and my Lord." ## Day 37 · Book 2 · Week 6

"Is this how you repay the Lord, you disgraceful, unwise people?! Is He not your Father, your Master? He has made you and established you." (Deuteronomy 32:6) That is ingratitude, after everything — the generation of the Flood, who managed to forget who made them. The haftorah stays in that water: "Bands of those that shall inherit the nether world have surrounded me; the snares of death confronted me." (II Samuel 22:6)

The quality is awe, *eimah*. After the Flood Noah felt it, having watched a world end. The Temple inspires it too.

It is worth separating awe from fear, which is next week's quality. *Yirah* is the ordinary word for fear: what you feel facing an immediate danger, or walking into an interview. *Eimah* is lower and longer — the dull, gnawing dread of something not yet visible, what a person feels marching toward a war. Studying Torah holds both. The Machzor Vitri says a student feels the direct fear of the teacher in whose presence he sits, and a more general awe at being in G-d's presence with G-d's Torah, which he must not misunderstand.

Yaakov had yirah at the place that became the Temple: "How awesome is this place! This is none other than the house of G-d, and this is the gate of heaven." Moshe had it at the bush: "Remove your shoes from your feet, for the place upon which you stand is holy soil" — and he hid his face, because he was afraid to look.

The prophet is Yehoshua, who has the same scene and reacts differently. Outside Jericho he meets a man with a drawn sword, asks "Are you for us, or for our adversaries?", and is told: "No, but I am the captain of the host of the Lord." He falls on his face, and is told to take off his shoe, for the place is holy. The Torah does not say he was afraid. He was Moshe's disciple and used to revelation — and this was the archangel Michael, not G-d Himself at the bush. The Talmud says the angel came in part because Yehoshua was neglecting Torah study at night when he was not actually fighting, which may be why the sword was out.

The Levitical city is Gath-Rimmon, the winepress of pomegranates. In Cheshvan we are pressed, to fill ourselves with as much good as a pomegranate holds seeds.

And that is the lesson: going with the flow is sometimes not enough. You have to press yourself, and know how much more there is to do.

## Day 38 · Book 3 · Week 6

"Your right hand, O Lord, is most powerful; Your right hand, O Lord, crushes the foe." The haftorah: "also the clouds dripped water. The mountains melted at the presence of the Lord." Clouds dripping and mountains melting — the Flood is still over this month.

Sotah's sixth daf takes up forbidden marriages, which were among the Flood's causes, and the bitter waters that would punish or clear a woman accused when her witnesses could not be brought.

The generation is Yered, who looks like a kosher Irad — the same name without the *ayin*. Ayin means an eye, and part of what undid Cain was the way he looked at his brother's accepted offering; sins of this kind begin with the eye. Ayin also means nothing, and Ecclesiastes says the difference between a person and a beast is *ayin*, nothing — read deeper, the ability to hold yourself as nothing, as part of the Infinite Light. Perhaps Yered made his ayin into such thorough nothingness that it stopped appearing in his name.

Cain's line runs Enoch, Mehujael, Irad — education, then a profane life, then descent, going downhill. Seth's line runs the other way: Mahalalel, Yered, Enoch — praise, then lowering yourself, in order to teach the next one. The results speak for themselves. Cain's descendants found better and better ways to be depraved and better weapons to kill with, until Cain was killed by one. Yered taught his son well, and that Enoch walked with G-d and was taken so the world's corruption could not reach him; his son Metushelach lived longest of anyone and died before the Flood came.

*Yered* and *Irad* both come from *laredet*, to go down. Which is this month's work: coming down from the heights of Tishrei to make this world a home.

The journey arrives somewhere good: "They journeyed from Marah and arrived in Elim, and in Elim there were twelve springs of water and seventy palm trees." Twelve springs for twelve tribes, seventy palms for seventy nations, and the tribes' water is what waters the palms. Elim is the strength that comes up out of bitterness — the same letters as the Name written with a *hay*, reordered to read *eli mah*, the hidden dimension of love, which emerges from inside the dark.

Exile has mixed everything up — which tribe a Jew belongs to, which nation a man comes from. *Bilbul*, a mix-up, is the root of *Bavel*, and the Talmud we learn is the Bavli: by logic and the rules of reading, confusion is sorted and put back where it belongs. *Mabul*, the Flood, is the same root, because the Flood mixed everything too.

Yered's lesson for prayer is humility, which is in his name. The one who leads is called *Yored Lifnei HaTeivah*, he who goes down before the Ark; the place he stands was lower than the room, and the law forbids praying from a raised place. Prayer comes from underneath: out of the depths I called You.

## Day 39 · Book 4 · Week 6

"And her rival vexed her sore, to make her fret, because the Lord had shut up her womb." Channah's story is Rachel's again — childless, and watching her sister bear one child after another. Rachel's yahrzeit is the eleventh of Cheshvan.

The week's quality is *Ohev et HaBri'ot*, loves people — more exactly, loves the creatures. It belongs to Aharon: "Be of the disciples of Aharon — a lover of peace, a pursuer of peace, loves the creatures and draws them close to Torah." Our sages say *creatures* is the word used because Aharon loved the people who had nothing else obviously good about them except that G-d had made them. In Cheshvan, out in the world, we meet exactly those people, and the work is to love them and learn from them.

Which is Channah's situation precisely. She lived beside a rival who taunted her for being childless, and that took real love of people and a real willingness to judge her favourably. We are told Peninah's motive was actually good — she wanted to sharpen Channah's prayers.

Proverbs' sixth chapter is a chapter about dealing with other people, and about learning from creatures that are not people at all. On standing surety for a stranger: "you have been trapped by the sayings of your mouth." On getting free: "Save yourself like a deer from the hand and like a bird from the hand of the snare." On industry: "Go to the ant, you sluggard; see her ways and become wise." And a portrait you would recognise anywhere: "An unscrupulous man, a man of violence, walks with a crooked mouth; he winks with his eyes, shuffles with his feet, points with his fingers."

The seventh of Cheshvan is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Yehuda Meir Shapiro of Lublin, who made the Daf Yomi cycle an institution — a devoted chassid of the Chortoker Rebbe, chief rabbi of Galina and Piotrkov, author of Or HaMeir, and founder of Yeshivat Chachmei Lublin in 1930. He had to argue the Jewish leadership of his day into Daf Yomi, partly against his own youth. It is now the measure of Talmud study across the world.

The ninth is the yahrzeit of Rabbi Yidele Horowitz, the Dzikover Rebbe, raised by his grandfather the Vizhnitzer. A formidable scholar who could not stand being treated as a rebbe, lived frugally, and gave away every penny sent to him by his chassidim, to orphans and widows.

## Day 40 · Book 5 · Week 6

"Behold, you are comely, my beloved, yea pleasant; also our couch is leafy. The beams of our houses are cedars; our corridors are cypresses." Then a new chapter opens: "I am a rose of Sharon, a rose of the valleys."

The first two are ours, and they are about Him resting among us — the Temple again. Rashi reads *yea pleasant* as His overlooking our transgression and letting His Shechinah rest in our midst, which is the praise of the fire coming down, "and all the people saw and shouted for joy." *Our couch is leafy* he reads as leafy with sons and daughters, all gathered to Him. Both the Tabernacle and the Temple are called a bed, because they are the source of Israel's fruitfulness. *The beams of our houses are cedars* is the praise of the Tabernacle — and the cypresses of the next line are the wood the Ark was built from.

*Chavatzelet*, the rose, our sages read as *chav tzel*, the shade of love, which Betzalel built: the Temple itself.

Of the seventy souls the sixth is Shimon — of all Jacob's sons the one tied to violence, which is what brought the Flood. *Chamas* is the word, and Jacob says of Shimon and Levi that their weapons are weapons of chamas. The name Shimon itself comes from *shmiah*, hearing.

Shevuot's sixth daf is mostly the afflictions of the metzora, with a dispute about colour. The metzora stands for a heavy kind of guilt and for bad behaviour among people — *lashon hara*, evil speech, and listening to it.

Jeremiah's sixth chapter sets the same things side by side: "Cut the trees and cast on Jerusalem a siege mound; that is the city whose sins have been visited upon her, everywhere there is oppression in its midst. As a well lets its water flow, so has she let her evil flow; violence and spoil is heard therein before Me continually; sickness and wounds. Be corrected, O Jerusalem, lest My soul be alienated from you; lest I make you a desolation, a land uninhabited." Water, and violence heard, and a land left empty — which is the world after the Flood. And a correction that could still stop it.

## Day 43 · Book 1 · Week 7

Week seven is still Cheshvan, and the swallow cannot keep quiet: "So that my soul shall praise You, and shall not be silent, G-d my Lord – I shall give thanks to You forever." (Psalms 30:13)

The word for forever, *l'olam*, holds *olam*, world — and olam comes from *ehelem*, which means hidden, a mask. Which is the month exactly: by going into the world we uncover a Presence that was covered up until then.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Eliezer three things. The honour of your fellow should be as precious to you as your own, and do not be easy to anger. Repent one day before your death — and since nobody knows the day, that means repent daily. And warm yourself by the fire of the sages, but beware of being burned by its embers: "for their bite is the bite of a fox, their sting is the sting of a scorpion, their hiss is the hiss of a serpent, and all their words are like fiery coals."

The Flood began on the seventeenth of Cheshvan, which falls in this week or the next. It would not have happened if those people had repented one day before their deaths and treated their neighbours and their sages properly. And the Torah holds Noah to account as well, for not praying for the others: his neighbour's honour was not precious to him. Stealing was one of the Flood's great causes, and stealing begins with an eye looking jealously at what belongs to somebody else.

The pairing is Malchut shebeChesed, and this week is the yahrzeit of Rachel, who is malchut. She had a great deal of chesed too, and did the very thing Rabbi Eliezer asks: Jacob had worked seven years for her and had given her signs so that Laban could not switch brides on the wedding night — and when Rachel saw Leah under the canopy she gave her sister the signs rather than let her be humiliated.

Malchut is also speech, which is the swallow who cannot be silent. The Alter Rebbe ties malchut to *kavod*, honour or glory — the word in the swallow's own verse, and the word we say after the Shema: *Baruch Shem Kvod Malchuto L'Olam Va'ed*.

The swallow's lesson: recognise and thank Him, always. And instruments were last week. This week it has to be your own voice.

## Day 44 · Book 2 · Week 7

"Remember the days of old; reflect upon the years of other generations. Ask your father, and he will tell you; your elders, and they will inform you." (Deuteronomy 32:7) Rashi takes it straight to the water: the generation of Enosh, whom G-d inundated with the waters of the ocean, and the generation of the Flood, whom He washed away.

The haftorah is this week's own verse: "When I am in distress, I call upon the Lord, yes I call upon my G-d: and out of His abode He hears my voice, and my cry enters His ears." (II Samuel 22:7) Because this is the week of Rachel's yahrzeit, and the most famous line in all of Tanach about her is a voice being heard: "A voice is heard in Ramah, lamentation and bitter weeping: Rachel is weeping for her children and refuses to be comforted for her children, because they are away." And the answer: "Restrain your voice from weeping, and your eyes from tears; for your work will be rewarded, says G-d; and they will return from the land of the enemy. There is hope for your future." He hears her as He hears David.

The quality is fear, *yirah* — last week's awe, now nearer and sharper. The prophet is Pinchas, in whom yirah is the whole story: what he did about the tribes' missing fear of Heaven at ba'al peor is what made him worthy to be a Kohen, then Kohen Gadol, and then Elijah the Prophet.

The Levitical city is Golan of Bashan, also a city of refuge. Golan comes from *galut*, exile — which is Rachel's verse. And galut shares its root with *legalot*, to reveal. Exertion in exile is what brings the revelations, and that is why Abraham did not object to being told his children would be exiled and come out with great possessions. Those were not only material: *rechush*, possessions, has the same numerical value as *Torah*.

What fear asks of a student is plain enough. The Alter Rebbe explains in the Tanya that fear of G-d matters not only for the prohibitions but for the positive commandments too. It is usually fear and not love that provides the first push to get anything done.

## Day 45 · Book 3 · Week 7

"And with Your great pride You tear down those who rise up against You; You send forth Your burning wrath; it devours them like straw." The retribution of the Flood is still running through these verses.

The haftorah holds two things that turn out to be one: "this was at Sinai, because of the presence of the Lord, the G-d of Israel. In the days of Shamgar the son of Anath." Both are feminine. The Shechinah, the Divine Presence, is always spoken of as feminine, and often as Rachel in particular — and this is her week. Anath is the name of a warrior goddess in Canaanite myth, and if it is a woman's name here, this is one of the very few places in Tanach where a man is named for his mother rather than his father.

Sotah's seventh daf carries on with the woman suspected of adultery and the bitter waters. On the way she is given a chance to reconsider, and she is reminded how her own forefathers admitted what they had done, repented, and earned their place in the World to Come. Which belongs to this week too, and to the verse about asking your father.

The generation is Chanoch, a kosher version of Cain's son of the same name. Cain's Enoch had a city named after him and sank into civilisation, further and further from nature and from Hashem. Seth's Chanoch walked with G-d. Rabbi Shalom Arush explains that he spent most of his time apart, so the world could not get at him, but came back regularly to teach — and in the end was taken alive, while he was still pure. The root of his name is *chinuch*, education, which is a parent's work.

The journey turns back to the water: "They journeyed from Elim and camped by the Red Sea." Last week was the twelve springs and the seventy palms, the tribes and the nations, both scattered and mixed together in exile. This week is standing at the shore, ready for the splitting. And on the week of Rachel Imeinu's yahrzeit the exile is felt more sharply, which is part of lifting it.

Chanoch's lesson for prayer is education. Pirkei Avot says an ignoramus cannot be pious. Beside Him we are all ignorant, and prayer is not mainly an intellectual thing — but there are still basics in how a person approaches the King, and anyone who can learn them should. Intent is what matters most, and intent is much harder when you do not understand what you are saying.

## Day 46 · Book 4 · Week 7

"And as he did so year by year, when she went up to the house of the Lord, so she vexed her; therefore she wept, and would not eat." Channah weeping, and going up each year — this is Rachel's week, whose crying is heard, and Cheshvan, which belongs to the Temple.

The week's quality is *Messameach et haMakom*, which reads as making the Place happy. He is called the Place because nowhere is without Him — and there is one place where the Presence gathers more than anywhere else, which is the Temple.

Where you are matters enormously. The Rambam writes that one of the ways open to a person who wants to turn from what he has done is to move house. There is a notion that changing place changes your *mazal*, and that is part of Avraham leaving his father's country for the land. Channah does not stay put either; she goes up to weep and pray for a child, rather than sit in the place where she is being taunted.

But the quality turns it around. Once a person is learning Torah for its own sake, it is no longer the place's job to make him happy — it is his job to make the place happy. We can make a Torah atmosphere wherever we are, which the sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe pressed hard, and the Rebbe after him. Even under a barrage like Channah's, how you behave is still yours and not the room's.

Proverbs' seventh chapter is another warning about strange places: "She is bustling and rebellious; her feet do not dwell in her house. Sometimes she is in the street, sometimes in the squares, and she lurks at every corner… Let your heart not veer off into her ways; stray not in her paths. For many are the dead that she has felled."

The eleventh of Cheshvan carries two more yahrzeits besides Rachel's, both of them first in lines that are still giving light. Rabbi Menachem Nachum, the Maggid of Chernobyl, a close disciple of the Ba'al Shem Tov and of the Maggid of Mezeritch, who wrote Meor Einayim — and Chernobyl, a name now attached to disaster, is also a name attached to great holiness. And Rabbi Avraham Wienberg, the Slonimer Rebbe, disciple of Rabbi Noah of Lechovitch and Rabbi Moshe of Kobrin, who wrote Yesod Ha'Avodah, and who was respected by chassidim and their opponents alike in Lithuania, where Chassidic thought was not welcome.

## Day 47 · Book 5 · Week 7

"As a rose among the thorns, so is my beloved among the daughters." That one is His. ## Day 50 · Book 1 · Week 8

Week eight is the end of Cheshvan, and the swift says one short thing: "My help is from G-d, Maker of Heaven and Earth." (Psalms 121:2)

It is the right verse for how far into the month we are. The deeper in we go the more we are immersed in things to be got done — sometimes sinking in them — and we need His help to stay afloat and not lose sight of what we were doing all this for.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Yehoshua on what ruins a person: "An evil eye, the evil inclination, and the hatred of one's fellows, drive a person from the world." Sin takes you out of the world. Though the phrase can be read the other way as well: turning back after sin takes a person higher than this material world reaches.

All three were loose in the generation of the Flood. The Torah singles out theft as what was most common then — and theft is the evil eye acting, wanting what is not yours. They were so taken up with things that ethics and the spirit stopped seeming important.

The pairing is Chesed shebeGevurah, kindness inside severity. The Flood came on slowly, which gave people room to turn back even after the rain had started. And it was not all destruction: it washed the world and let it begin again. Forty days and forty nights, which is also the forty measures of water a mikvah needs to purify.

From the swift we learn that we need Him for everything, in everything. If you feel alone and past helping, do what this bird does and ask.

## Day 51 · Book 2 · Week 8

"When the Most High gave nations their lot, when He separated the sons of man, He set up the boundaries of peoples according to the number of the children of Israel." (Deuteronomy 32:8) Rashi reads both halves as this month's history: giving those who angered Him their portion means He flooded them and drowned them; and separating the sons of man is the scattering of the generation that built the tower — whom He could have removed from the world altogether and did not. He set boundaries instead, and let them live.

The haftorah is the same ground shaking: "Then the earth shook and quaked, the very foundations of heaven did tremble; and they were shaken when he was angered." (II Samuel 22:8)

The quality is humility, *anavah*, and real humility means caring about someone besides yourself. Noah failed that test. Moshe, whom the Torah calls the humblest man on earth, passed it — and is a tikkun for Noah. Told that He would destroy the people and build a nation out of him, which is very nearly what Noah was told, Moshe answered: erase me from Your book. The Hebrew, *M'cheni Nah*, has the same letters as *Mei Noach*, the waters of Noah, which is what the prophets call the Flood.

The prophets this week are Elkanah and Channah, who are prayer and the Temple, which is the month. Both are humility. From Channah we learn how to pray — whispering, so that nobody hears but herself and Hashem. And Elkanah, for all his own level, was worried about his whole generation: he made the pilgrimage to Shiloh every year by a different route so that more people would come with him, at a time when nobody was bothering, and even though the priests serving there had become corrupt. Their son Samuel was the one who lifted the people.

The Levitical city is Be'eshterah, another name for Ashtarot — which appears to be named for Ashtoreth, a goddess that King Solomon is said to have worshipped. The commentators say he did not; his wives did, and his not stopping them counted against him as if he had. He built the first Temple and finished it in this month. And Ashtarot seems to be bound up with idol worship about wealth, which was Solomon's own exposure, being rich — and is ours in a smaller way, going into this month and risking drowning in things.

So: be careful not to pick up false gods out there, or false ideas, or the rationalisations that go with them. Solomon also wrote that many waters cannot extinguish the love. The many waters are the difficulty of making a living, and however deep they get, they cannot put it out.

## Day 52 · Book 3 · Week 8

"And with the breath of Your nostrils the waters were heaped up; the running water stood erect like a wall." Water standing up — the sea and the Flood alongside each other again.

The haftorah brings in another woman, which has been this fortnight's thread: "in the days of Jael, caravans ceased, and travelers walked on crooked paths." Those crooked paths, *arachot akalkalot*, have at their root *lekalkel*, to ruin — which is what had taken hold of the human race before the Flood.

Sotah's eighth daf continues with the bitter waters, and takes care that the testing itself should not provoke illicit thoughts about other men. It also treats Divine retribution in general, which comes *middah k'neged middah*, measure for measure.

The generation is Methuselah, a kosher Methushael. He was righteous, and he lived longer than anyone in history — nine hundred and sixty-nine years — and he died immediately before the Flood came. His yahrzeit falls in this month, on the same day as Rachel's.

Both names open with *met*, dead. But Methuselah continues with *shalach*, sent, and Methushael with *sha'al*, asked or borrowed, which is also *she'ol*, the grave. One name sends death away; the other brings it closer. Methuselah's life was life-affirming and his predecessor's was not. He kept his righteousness in an environment thoroughly corrupted, in a way that was itself above nature — which is the task in exile too.

The journey is into hunger: "They journeyed from the Red Sea and camped in the desert of Zin." That is where the provisions carried out of Egypt ran out. They cried, and the manna came. The Torah says there was no water either, and water also stands for Torah. We are at the same point in the year: what Tishrei supplied is running down, and we have to ask hard for what will keep us going. In Kislev it comes the way the manna came, from outside nature altogether. One meaning of *Zin* is moon, and a new moon is as empty as that desert.

Methuselah's lesson for prayer is to affirm being alive. Before anything else, praise and thanks for being here, in the way the Creator meant. As the Torah puts it: choose life. That is nearly the only decision there is, and it comes up every day.

## Day 53 · Book 4 · Week 8

"And Elkanah her husband said unto her: 'Hannah, why weepest thou? and why eatest thou not? and why is thy heart grieved? am not I better to thee than ten sons?'" Here is somebody stepping in to try to make her happy.

The further we get from Tishrei the more bogged down the world's business makes us feel, and it can be a great deal at once. Being able to reach somebody who will lift you is worth something. And the week's quality is exactly that: *Messame'ach et HaBri'ot*, one who makes the creatures happy.

Elkanah's three questions are a short course in how. Sometimes you have to remind a person that they are a created being with the same ordinary needs as every other one — why aren't you eating? Then point at what is good: am I not better to you than ten sons? And then the thing that is hardest to hear and truest, that it looks this bad only from where we are standing, and the Creator has a plan for each of us in which everything He does is for the best.

Proverbs' eighth chapter is wisdom speaking in her own voice, and most of it is about creation. "The Lord acquired me at the beginning of His way, before His works of old. From the distant past I was enthroned, from the beginning, of those that preceded the earth. I was created when there were yet no deeps, when there were no fountains replete with water… When He established the heavens, there I was, when He drew a circle over the face of the deep… when He gave the sea its boundary, and the water shall not transgress His command." And then the lines that make the chapter belong to this week: "I was a nursling beside Him, and I was His delight every day, playing before Him at all times; playing in the habitable world of His earth, and having my delights with the children of man."

The twenty-first of Cheshvan is the yahrzeit of two great Sefardi kabbalists. The Radbaz, Rabbi David ben Zimra, Chief Rabbi of Egypt from about 1514 to 1553, who taught the Arizal and Rabbi Betzalel Ashkenazi, and was a wealthy businessman besides. And Rabbi Avraham Azulai, author of the Chessed L'Avraham, who also shaped the Arizal and is quoted often in his works — Chief Rabbi of Hebron, and the great-great-grandfather of the Chidah.

## Day 54 · Book 5 · Week 8

"Sustain me with flagons of wine, spread my bed with apples, for I am lovesick. His left hand was under my head, and his right hand would embrace me. I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles or by the hinds of the field, that you neither awaken nor arouse the love while it is desirous."

Two of these are ours to Him and the third is addressed to the nations. Rashi takes the first literally as nursing: sustain me now as one does the sick, with flagons of grape wine or cakes of white flour; spread my bed around me with apples for the good smell, in the way of the sick — for I am sick for his love, and I thirst for Him here in my exile. That is this month. The deeper into the year, the more entrenched in daily business, and the sicker for what we are missing.

The second verse remembers when it was easier to see. Rashi: His left hand was under my head — in the desert; and His right hand would embrace me — He went a three days' journey to find them rest, and where the rest was He brought down manna and quails. All of which I remember now in exile, and I am sick for His love. The left hand is discipline, laid under the head to keep us in line; the right is kindness, in an embrace. Which is this week's pairing exactly, kindness inside severity.

The third verse turns outward and is less yielding: whatever the weight of the physical world just now, we are not giving up what we have with Him.

There is the Flood in these verses too. Noah comes out of the ark and back into the world and is overwhelmed by it; he plants a vineyard and falls much as Adam did. One of the major opinions in the Talmud is that the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge was the grape.

Of the seventy souls the eighth is Jamin — *yemin*, the right hand, which is the kindness above. Which is what this part of the year leans on more and more.

Shevuot's eighth daf stays with the goat whose blood went into the Holy of Holies, but the sins have got graver: idolatry, certain sexual sins, murder — though the Talmud is speaking of them done unwittingly. The deeper into exile, the more atonement is needed.

Jeremiah's eighth chapter is the same descent: sins mounting, worse ones, and the eye for how bad they are going dim. They become ordinary, then acceptable, and at last we think we are doing His will. The chapter happens to name the birds of this season: "Even the stork in the heaven knows her seasons, and the turtledoves and the crane and the swallow await the time of their coming, but My people do not know the ordinance of the Lord." And: "they healed the breach of My people easily, saying, 'Peace, peace,' but there is no peace."

## Day 57 · Book 1 · Week 9

Week nine, and the stormy petrel announces: "Light is sown for the righteous, and joy for the straight-hearted." (Psalms 97:11)

Some years this week sits entirely in Cheshvan; other years it already takes in the first of Kislev, the month of Chanukah. Even when Rosh Chodesh Kislev falls later, this week holds a date the Maccabees kept: the twenty-third of Cheshvan, which in the time of the Talmud was much celebrated, because that was when the stones of the altar that the Greeks had made impure were carried out.

Light, and sowing, and protection for the righteous. The Maccabees were righteous fighters of the seed of Aaron, and G-d arranged it that they beat Greece, which was the greatest empire there was.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Yossi: "The property of your fellow should be as precious to you as your own. Perfect yourself for the study of Torah, for it is not an inheritance to you. And all your deeds should be for the sake of Heaven."

Inheritance is the word that ties it to Kislev. The Greeks admired the Torah as a philosophy and liked its practical parts — respecting another man's money among them — but they wanted our link to it cut, and our own connection to G-d with it. The Midrash puts it exactly: "darkness symbolizes Greece, which darkened the eyes of Israel with its decrees, ordering Israel to, 'Write on the horn of an ox that you have no inheritance in the G-d of Israel.'" Rabbi Yossi was himself a kohen, like the Maccabees, and like them he is called a chassid — someone who goes past the letter of the law to do what G-d wants.

For Rabbi Yossi the thing that matters most is a good neighbour, and avoiding a bad one at all costs. A good neighbour, *shachen tov*, may be the Shechinah, which lives among the Jewish people and in the Temple. The bad one is the empire that worked so hard to make us like itself.

The pairing is Gevurah shebeGevurah, severity within severity.

The petrel's lesson is that a long way towards being happy is simply being a good, honest, fair person. Rebbe Nachman teaches to look for those good points and dwell on them — in others and in yourself. Even when they are small and incomplete, they are worth real joy.

## Day 58 · Book 2 · Week 9

"Because the Lord's portion is His people Jacob, the lot of His inheritance." (Deuteronomy 32:9) Inheritance again, which is the week's whole subject and the Greeks' particular target — the decree that Israel write on the horn of an ox that they have no inheritance in the G-d of Israel.

The haftorah is fire: "Smoke went up in His nostrils, and fire out of His mouth did devour; coals flamed forth from Him." (II Samuel 22:9) The miracle of Chanukah is light and fire. And coals are a good figure for the Jews of that time, because a coal can be cold on the outside and hot inside, and all that is needed is to get the heat out. That was true then and it is true now, when Jews of every kind come out of the woodwork to light together. The verse is also about His might, which is the other half of that miracle — the military one.

The quality is joy, *simchah*. The spiritual kind is one of the great subjects of Chanukah, in praise and in thanks; it is also central to the Temple and to the work of the Kohanim.

The prophet is Eli, the Kohen Gadol, whose service had to be done in joy despite what his life handed him — including his own sons' corruption. Which rhymes with Chanukah, where part of the fight was against the corrupting pull of Hellenist culture.

Because this week lies about half the time in Cheshvan, whose four Levitical cities have all been given, its city is one of the three cities of refuge still to come — likely in the territory of the half-tribe of Menasheh, across the Jordan. And since half the time this week already belongs to Kislev, whose tribe is Benjamin, it is also Jerusalem: half the Temple's area, itself a city of refuge, lies in Benjamin, though in time Jerusalem will be its own territory and no tribe's. It fits either way. Cheshvan saw the first Temple finished and will see the third dedicated; Kislev holds the finishing of the Mishkan and the rededication of the second.

What joy asks is simple and hard. It is not meant to depend on your circumstances. Being happy is first of all a decision — and for the merit of making it, He will give you plenty to be happy about.

## Day 59 · Book 3 · Week 9

"The depths congealed in the heart of the sea. Because the enemy said, I will pursue." The verse breaks in half, and so does the week: the first part is still the Flood, the second starts something new — a pursuing enemy, which is Chanukah's subject.

The haftorah splits the same way: "The open cities ceased, in Israel they ceased, until I Deborah arose; I arose as a mother in Israel." Open cities ceasing is civilisation stopping, as in the Flood. Then Devorah stands up, as a mother. Her war against Sisera runs parallel to the Maccabees' — and a mother's deepest job is keeping her child's identity intact, which is why Chanukah comes from *chinuch*, education. These weeks have been full of women: Rachel, Anath, Yael, and now Devorah.

Sotah's ninth daf also divides: the bitter waters, and then Shimshon. Samson belongs to Teveth with the tribe of Dan, but Kislev's subject is in him anyway — he was meant to be pure, and what undid him was impurity, intermarriage among it. Teveth holds several days of Chanukah too.

The generation is Lamech, Noah's father, a kosher version of Cain's Lamech, who killed Cain and his own son besides. Cain's Lamech destroys his ancestor and his child; Seth's Lamech, the moment his son is born, says the world will be comforted through him — and through that son his whole line, and the human race, is saved.

Both Lamechs have speeches recorded. Cain's: "Adah and Zillah, hearken to my voice… for I have slain a man by wounding him and a child by bruising him." Seth's: "And he named him Noah, saying, 'This one will give us rest from our work and from the toil of our hands from the ground, which the Lord has cursed.'"

Cain's Lamech also stands for aesthetics and pleasure worshipped without check, which is what Greek culture was at the time, and for technology used the same way. Rashi says that before Noah there were no plowshares, and he made them. Cain's line built better weapons, until Cain himself was killed by one; Seth's built tools that made life easier. Beauty and science are good things held inside something larger, which is what Noah says: "May God expand Japheth, and may he dwell in the tents of Shem." Japheth — whose name comes from *yoffi*, beauty, and from whom Yavan, Greece, descends — may grow; in the tents of Shem, whose name means *name*.

The journey is a knock: "They journeyed from the desert of Zin and camped in Dophkah." From *dofek*, to knock. It is the new moon coming, and the knocking in the chest — *Kol Dodi Dofek*, the voice of my beloved knocking. Providence was ready to put everything back as it had been, if they had all been willing to return; only some were, and the majority and the notables stayed in Babylon, preferring dependence, unwilling to leave their houses and their affairs. Which is the risk of exile: getting comfortable.

Lamech's lesson for prayer is to reach Him above reason. In *melech*, king, the mem is *moach*, the mind, and the lamed is *lev*, the heart — and the mind is meant to rule the heart. Swap the two letters and melech becomes *lemech*, a fool. And yet there is also *shtus d'kedusha*, holy folly: prayer is the service of the heart and not the mind, and what we have with Him is above nature and above logic.

## Day 60 · Book 4 · Week 9

"So Hannah rose up after they had eaten in Shiloh, and after they had drunk — now Eli the priest sat upon his seat by the door-post of the temple of the Lord." Hannah rising, and the next figure in the story arriving: Eli, the Kohen Gadol. Kislev and Chanukah belong to the Kohen Gadol and to the priests. Rashi notes Eli had been made judge over all Israel — and in the Chanukah story the Chashmona'im were priests and kings at once.

And Eli is sitting by the door-post, the *mezuzah*, of the Temple. Which is precisely where the Chanukah lamp is put.

The week's quality is that the Torah enclothes him with humility. It is a strange pairing, because clothes — and particularly the kind one would associate with the Torah — sound like honour, not lowliness. The High Priest's garments are described as being for honour and beauty. But the honour and the beauty are not his. They belong to Hashem and to the office, and the splendour of them is exactly what makes the man wearing them feel small. The Torah works the same way: the more you learn of how great He is and how deep it goes, the smaller you get.

Rashi finds it in the spelling. "And Eli the priest was sitting on the chair" is written defectively, missing a letter, because that day he sat on a huge chair, having been appointed judge over Israel. The chair makes the office great and the man small. And he sat outside, where he could greet whoever came in, which is its own kind of humility.

Proverbs' ninth chapter splits in two. The first part is wisdom keeping house: "Wisdom has built her house; she has hewn her seven pillars. She has prepared her meat; she has mingled her wine; she has even set her table… 'Whoever is simple, let him turn in here'… 'Come, partake of my bread and drink of the wine I have mingled.'" Rashi reads the house as the world and the seven pillars as the seven days of creation. The rest of the chapter returns to corruption and temptation. And buried in it is the Jewish answer to everything Greece offered: "The beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord, and the knowledge of the holy ones is understanding." The Greeks were wisdom itself, and not that.

The twenty-seventh of Cheshvan is the yahrzeit of Reb Yaakov Leizer, second Rebbe of Pshevorsk, who based himself in Antwerp. He had hundreds of chassidim and never left off his plain ways: he respected the lowliest Jews, often slept on a bench in the ezras nashim so a wandering beggar could have his bed, and slept in his sukkah with tens of others who had none.

And the twenty-ninth is the yahrzeit of Rebbe Tzvi-Hirsch of Riminov, who was his predecessor's personal attendant before he was his successor — known as Rav Tzvi Hersch Meshares, the helper — and later as a worker of miracles, whose Torah was gathered in Be'erot HaMayim.

## Day 61 · Book 5 · Week 9

"The sound of my beloved! Behold, he is coming, skipping over the mountains, jumping over the hills. My beloved resembles a gazelle or a fawn of the hinds; behold, he is standing behind our wall, looking from the windows, peering from the lattices. My beloved raised his voice and said to me, 'Arise, my beloved, my fair one, and come away.'"

All three are ours, and all three share their second word: *dodi*, my Beloved. What they describe is rescue that does not go by the rules — skipping over mountains, jumping hills — which is Kislev and the Maccabees, where He saves in ways nature does not allow. Which is also the difference between the two systems in that war. Jews want a close relationship with Him and call Him beloved; the Greeks of the time believed in the cosmos and its laws, and in no Judge at all.

And "standing behind our wall, looking from the windows, peering from the lattices" is a known reference to the blessing of the priests — Kislev and Chanukah again.

Of the seventy souls the ninth is Ohad, spelled with a *hey* where *echad*, one, has a *chet* — and monotheism was the other great dividing line. Rashi notes that the family of Ohad was one of the few wiped out entirely: when Aharon died the clouds of glory withdrew and the Canaanites came to fight, and the people set their hearts on going back to Egypt and went back eight stages of the journey. The Levites pursued them to bring them back and killed seven of their families, losing four of their own. Which has the shape of the Chanukah story too, where part of the fighting was Jew against Jew, those who meant to stay Jews against those who did not.

Shevuot's ninth daf stays with the goat offered outside the Temple, and takes up the goats of Rosh Chodesh and the festivals. Rosh Chodesh, with Shabbat and circumcision, was one of the three the Greeks tried to forbid outright. It stands for His never-ending part in how we live: for all that we now use a fixed calendar, the ideal was looking up for the first thin appearance of the new moon — a reminder that even the part of our lives tied to nature's cycles is in His hands.

Jeremiah's ninth chapter arrives at lines everybody knows: "Let not the wise man boast of his wisdom, nor the strong man boast of his strength, nor the rich man boast of his riches. But let him that boasts exult in this, that he understands and knows me, for I am the Lord Who practices kindness, justice and righteousness on the earth." Wise, strong, rich — the Greeks were all three, and their wisdom had no G-d in it. Which is what we say in the Shemoneh Esreh: the strong into the hands of the weak, the many into the hands of the few, the impure into the hands of the pure. The chapter ends on circumcision, and on hearts that are uncircumcised.

## Day 64 · Book 1 · Week 10

Week ten, and the bat repeats something G-d said: "Comfort My people, comfort them, says your Lord." (Isaiah 40:1)

Now we are fully into Kislev, whose tribe is Benjamin — known for keeping Jewish values intact for the generations after him, and for how much he was prepared to give up. The bat can see in the dark, which is the useful trick in a month of long cold nights. And it is in this month, at Chanukah, that the comfort actually arrives: the Greeks' darkness is beaten and the light of the Temple is put back.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Shimon: "Be meticulous with the reading of the Shemah and with prayer. When you pray, do not make your prayers routine, but an entreaty of mercy and a supplication before the Almighty… And do not be wicked in your own eyes."

The Shema is the plainest statement of there being One, and of this people taking Him as King. Prayer is the intimacy with Him. Those two are exactly what the Greeks wanted gone. They could live with a cosmos — cold, indifferent to how anyone behaves — but not with a G-d who is a merciful Father and a King.

Rabbi Shimon's own answer, asked what path to take, was to see what is coming — literally, to see what is being born — and his worst thing was borrowing and not repaying, because one who borrows from a friend borrows from G-d. Seeing what is about to happen is one of the Talmud's definitions of real wisdom. The Greeks had wisdom. Wisdom by itself is not enough: it has to be tied to the ethics that come with One G-d. And not repaying a loan is not only dishonest, it throws back the mercy somebody showed you, which was a piece of His.

The pairing is Tiferet shebeGevurah — and tiferet is also called *rachamim*, mercy. Further from Tishrei now, we still remember how beautiful it was, and we ask for mercy to hold on to what we decided then.

The bat's lesson: pray for mercy, and hold up the people around you — the poor and the pressed-upon first.

## Day 65 · Book 2 · Week 10

"He found them in a desert land, and in a desolate, howling wasteland. He encompassed them and bestowed understanding upon them; He protected them as the pupil of His eye." (Deuteronomy 32:10) A desolation, and understanding given inside it. That was the state of things at the time of the Chanukah miracle — and what He gave them was not only protection from being absorbed, but the very tools of logic and argument that Greek thought had, to be used on the Torah. Much of the reasoning in the Babylonian Talmud came out of that meeting. The Talmud is full of Greek words, and the name of the Greek king Ptolomy, *Talmai*, has the same numerical value as *Talmud*.

The haftorah bends the sky: "And He bent the heavens and He came down; and thick darkness was under His feet." (II Samuel 22:10) Coming down and bending what nature does — which is what the Jews saw then. And the Greek exile is compared to darkness, while this festival is the one of light.

The quality is purity, *taharah*. With last week's joy, those two are the qualities of Chanukah: after the Greeks defiled the Temple the Kohanim purified it and dedicated it again, and the miracle turns on pure oil still carrying the Kohen Gadol's seal.

The prophet is Samuel, who is purity itself. A Nazir from before he was born, raised in the Tabernacle by the Kohen Gadol, spoken to by G-d while he was still a child — and his greatness is reckoned as Moshe and Aharon together.

The Levitical city is Gibeon, where something else was bent. "Then Joshua spoke to the Lord on the day when the Lord delivered up the Amorites before the children of Israel, and he said in the sight of Israel, 'Sun, stand still upon Gibeon, and Moon in the valley of Ayalon.' And the sun stood still, and the moon stayed, until the people had avenged themselves upon their enemies." Night was coming and the pursuit would have had to stop; the sun staying put gave them the light and the hours. The whole world saw it.

What purity asks of a student is a mind clear enough to take the thing in. A head full of a million useless or degrading images cannot absorb Torah properly, and if the animal in a person is loose it is hard to concentrate and harder to feel what is holy. Without some purifying, the line between holy and ordinary blurs until nothing seems special at all. If everything is holy then nothing is. A yes is only worth something if the same person sometimes says no.

## Day 66 · Book 3 · Week 10

"I will overtake, I will share the booty; my desire will be filled from them; I will draw my sword, my hand will impoverish them." That is the enemy talking, and confident — as the Greeks were at first about what looked like a small and simple Jewish revolt.

The haftorah names the cause: "When they chose new gods, then there was war in the cities; was there seen a shield or a spear." Assimilation, idolatry, and then being hunted — all three were in the Greek exile.

Sotah's tenth daf stays with Samson, and takes in Judah and Abshalom. Its subject is licentiousness, and children brought up badly who turn against their parents. Which belongs to this month, since Chanukah comes from *chinuch*, education.

The tenth generation from Adam is Noah, who lived in a society that had gone entirely rotten — much as the Jews did under the Greeks. Pirkei Avot says that He waited ten generations before bringing the Flood, out of patience.

## Day 67 · Book 4 · Week 10

"And she was in bitterness of soul — and prayed unto the Lord, and wept sore." Hannah praying out of bitterness, in the week the bat sings of comfort. The verse says she prayed *al* Hashem, upon Him, above the level of revelation that the Name itself is. In Kislev too, the connection runs very high.

The week's quality is that the Torah enclothes him with fear — following last week's humility. The two go together, and they are together in the list of forty-eight as well. What Kislev and Chanukah ask for involves both.

Hannah's weeping is the fear. She had been made to feel so small and so insignificant that it brought her to a greater fear of G-d, and that is what let her pray with such complete self-effacement that no words came out of her mouth at all. She was entirely nullified before the Master.

Proverbs' tenth chapter is the righteous set against the wicked, line after line, and much of it is about the mouth. "Treasures of wickedness will not avail, but charity will save from death… Blessings shall come upon the head of a righteous man, but violence shall cover the mouth of the wicked. The mention of a righteous man is for a blessing, but the name of the wicked shall rot… He who covers up hatred has false lips, and he who spreads slander is a fool. In a multitude of words, transgression will not be avoided, and he who holds back his lips is wise. The tongue of the righteous is choice silver… Fear of the Lord will add days, but the years of the wicked will be shortened."

The righteous few against the wicked many is Chanukah's own phrase. And it is worth noticing that this month's two Chassidic holidays, Yud Kislev and Yud-Tes Kislev, both mark a Rebbe of Chabad coming out of a prison he was put in by slander.

The week holds two yahrzeits of men who built Torah in America out of nothing hospitable. In America he founded the Nitra Yeshiva and a farming community that kept the Torah's agricultural laws.

## Day 68 · Book 5 · Week 10

"For behold, the winter has passed; the rain is over and gone. The blossoms have appeared in the land, the time of singing has arrived, and the voice of the turtledove is heard in our land. The fig tree has put forth its green figs, and the vines with their tiny grapes have given forth their fragrance; arise, my beloved, my fair one, and come away."

Winter into spring, which is rescue. Rashi reads it against Egypt and the Passover redemption, and the redemption of this month has much of that one in it. The rain being over is the Flood finally behind us. The singing is the pure singing of the Kohanim and the Maccabees.

And the third verse, Rashi takes apart: "The fig tree has put forth its green figs" — these are the transgressors of Israel, who perished during the three days of darkness; "and the vines with their tiny grapes gave forth their fragrance" — those who were left of them repented and were accepted. Figs and grapes are two of the fruits the Land is praised for, and after the Greeks were beaten, life in it began to come back to normal.

Of the seventy souls the tenth is Jachin, which means to establish or to prepare — and the verb is in our prayer for Jerusalem: *ve'chise David meheira le'tocha tachin*, and install within it soon the throne of David. There is a separate blessing for the kingdom, and yet the throne is named inside the rebuilding, because the Temple and the throne together are what the city is: a place united by worship and by governance. Jachin was also the name of one of the two pillars of Solomon's Temple; the other was Boaz.

Shevuot's tenth daf continues with the goat of the festivals, of Rosh Chodesh and of Yom Kippur, and asks whether the holiness of certain Temple items can leave them.

Jeremiah's tenth chapter keeps the nations' idols apart from Israel's portion: "Not like these is Jacob's portion, for He is the One Who formed everything, and Israel is the tribe of His inheritance; the Lord of Hosts is His name." ## Day 71 · Book 1 · Week 11

Week eleven, and the stork carries on where the bat left off: "Speak to the heart of Jerusalem, and call to her, for her time has arrived, for her sins have been pardoned, for she has taken double from G-d's hand for all her sins." (Isaiah 40:2) The punishment is over; the city has had double for everything.

And what is the heart of Jerusalem? Most likely the Temple itself. It was on Chanukah that the Temple was taken back, cleaned of impurity and dedicated again — and Chanukah means dedication.

This week holds Yud Kislev, when the second Rebbe of Lubavitch, the Mitteler Rebbe, was let out of prison. He had been arrested on charges invented out of nothing, that he was fomenting rebellion against the government — strikingly like the charges made against his father.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Elazar: "Be diligent in the study of Torah. Know what to answer a heretic. And know before whom you toil, and who is your employer who will repay you the reward of your labors."

Knowing what to answer an *apikores* is this month exactly, since what we are marking is having held off the parts of Greek philosophy that cut against the Torah. Epicureanism, with its eye on pleasure, is probably the strain furthest from it, and the one with the most appeal just then. And the other half of his saying — know before Whom you work, and who pays — is the direct connection the Greeks could not make sense of at all.

Rabbi Matis Weinberg points out that *Tzion* and *Yavan*, Zion and Greece, differ by a single letter: the *tzadik*. That is the difference — acting justly before G-d with a good heart, and being bound to Him and to the righteous of every generation. Which is why the Midrash has the Greeks demanding their heresy be written on the horn of an ox, the sign of Yosef HaTzadik.

The pairing is Netzach shebeGevurah. The first chapter of the Shulchan Aruch teaches that gevurah's own place is the heart, where a person can defeat his own enemy, the yetzer hara.

And the stork's lesson, which is harder than it sounds: learn to ask forgiveness properly, and learn to actually forgive. We are only here because of His forgiveness, handed out daily.

## Day 72 · Book 2 · Week 11

"As an eagle awakens its nest, hovering over its fledglings, it spreads its wings, taking them and carrying them on its pinions." (Deuteronomy 32:11) A parent waking its young and carrying them. One of the great subjects of Chanukah is *chinuch*, bringing children up — and we can see the pains the Alter Rebbe took over his own son and successor.

There is a Chassidic saying that Yud Kislev is the birth of the chassid and Yud Tes Kislev, next week, his circumcision. The two dates belong to each other, which says a good deal about the bond between the first two Rebbes of Chabad.

Both this week's verses are wings. The haftorah: "And He rode upon a cherub and did fly; He was seen upon the wings of the wind." (II Samuel 22:11) Flying belongs to a month whose holidays are above nature, and to the kind of men they are about.

And in the presence of a sage you not only get your questions answered — you find out how many questions you had not thought to ask.

## Day 73 · Book 3 · Week 11

"You blew with Your wind, the sea covered them; they sank like lead in the powerful waters." The word for wind is *ruach*, which is also spirit and soul. Spirit, and its strength, is what separated the Jews from the Greeks. And the sea stands for hidden wisdom, as water stands for Torah.

The haftorah: "when the forty thousand went against Israel? My heart is toward the lawgivers of Israel." Forty thousand of the enemy defeated, and a heart turned toward the wise men of Israel — which is Chanukah twice over: the sheer numbers, and where the victory came from, which in that case was the Kohanim. And it suits two lawgivers saved on Yud Kislev and Yud Tes Kislev.

Sotah's eleventh daf is Egypt — the enslavement, the persecution, and the brave work of Miriam and Yocheved, and the miraculous ways Jewish children were kept alive.

The eleventh generation from Adam is Shem, Noah's son, whose name means *name* and who stands for truth. Avraham comes from him, and all the Semitic peoples. His brother Yafeth's name means beauty, and Yavan, Greece, descends from him. So the Maccabean war with Greek culture is in fact a fight between the sons of Shem and of Yafeth.

The journey runs dry: "They journeyed from Alush and camped in Rephidim, but there there was no water for the people to drink." Rephidim comes from *rafeh*, weak, and points at devotion to Torah study gone slack. Last week was learning to hold power without being corrupted by it; this week is getting past the weakness that comes of having no water. That is Kislev's fight, and the Mitteler Rebbe's.

Shem's lesson is self-examination done truthfully — going back over the day, the week, the month, and seeing what can be better. *Cheshbon HaNefesh*, a spiritual accounting, and a central part of the practice called hitbodedut.

## Day 75 · Book 5 · Week 11

"My dove, in the clefts of the rock, in the coverture of the steps, show me your appearance, let me hear your voice, for your voice is pleasant and your appearance is comely." That one is His. Then ours: "Seize for us the foxes, the little foxes, who destroy the vineyards, for our vineyards are with tiny grapes. My beloved is mine, and I am his, who grazes among the roses."

The dove in the rock is this people crying out in purity when there is nowhere to go. Rashi puts it at the Red Sea, where the crying revealed what Israel is underneath — which is rather like a hidden flask of pure oil still carrying the Kohen Gadol's seal. And the voice is called *arev*, pleasant, which spells *orev*, a raven, next week's creature.

The little foxes that ruin vineyards: the fox stands for the Temple's destruction, and Rashi recalls the Egyptians throwing infant boys into the Nile — who ended up drowned themselves. Wine, like oil, means wisdom and Torah. The Greeks came for our knowledge at a moment when the people's learning was thin: infants, and tiny grapes.

The third verse is the whole relationship in a line, and Rashi makes it mutual and exclusive: "He demanded all His needs from me… All my needs I demanded of Him, and not of other deities." Which neither the rationalist nor the pagan Greek could follow.

Of the seventy souls the eleventh is Tzochar — also the name, in the Torah, of the father of Ephron the Hittite, who sold Avraham the Ma'arat HaMachpelah to bury Sarah. The tzohar, like the Menorah, lit what was outside it as well as what was within.

Shevuot's eleventh daf is the holiness of the ketoret and of what is dedicated — and the ketoret has eleven ingredients.

Jeremiah's eleventh chapter has Him telling the prophet to speak to Jerusalem, and returning again and again to the fathers — one generation holding to the next, which is the week's subject. And to the *brit*, the covenant, which the Greeks set themselves against: "Cursed be the man who will not hearken to the words of this covenant, which I commanded your forefathers on the day I took them out of the land of Egypt, out of the iron furnace."

## Day 78 · Book 1 · Week 12

Week twelve, and the raven asks: "Who prepares food for the raven, when his young ones cry out to G-d?" (Job 38:41)

This is the week of Yud Tes Kislev, called the Rosh Hashanah of Chassidism — the day the Alter Rebbe came out of prison, and the yahrzeit of the Maggid of Mezeritch. He had been accused of treason by the opponents of Chassidism, with the death penalty in prospect, and he came out of it. He took the release as a verdict in the Heavenly Court as well as the earthly one, and afterwards he taught far more openly. The effort that followed carried the warmth of Chassidism into the coldest and most indifferent part of a Jew: the intellect. The capital of intellectual Judaism was Vilna, where he had been sent.

The raven is the right creature for that. It was put out of Noah's ark for not keeping the rule of celibacy, and it is known for being cruel and careless toward its own young. And yet when Elijah fled from Ahab, G-d arranged that the raven — which does not even feed its own chicks — should bring him food. Elijah had run out of hope and was complaining about the state of the people, and the lesson was that like the raven, everyone has warmth and good in them; it only has to be brought out.

The word itself says so. *Orev*, raven, is close to *arev* — responsible for another, and sweet, and mixed together, as in *Kol Israel Arevim Zeh LaZeh*, that all of Israel is responsible for, sweet to and mixed up with one another. Which is nearly the whole basis of Chassidism and of the Torah: love your fellow as yourself.

Twelve is the twelve tribes. For all the differences and the setbacks, we are mixed together and answerable for each other. On his deathbed Jacob was worried about exactly those differences, and the Talmud says his twelve sons answered him in unison: "Listen, O Israel" — meaning Jacob — "Hashem is our G-d, Hashem is One."

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Tarfon: "The day is short, the work is much, the workers are lazy, the reward is great, and the Master is pressing." And: "It is not incumbent upon you to finish the task, but neither are you free to absolve yourself from it." Chassidism came to light a fire under people who were low and spiritually asleep. An alarm clock: the time is short, start now.

The pairing is Hod shebeGevurah. Hod belongs to the inner dimensions of the Torah — as Lag Ba'Omer, which is hod within hod, is the yahrzeit of the man who set down the Zohar.

## Day 79 · Book 2 · Week 12

"So the Lord guided them alone, and there was no alien deity with Him." (Deuteronomy 32:12) One of the first principles of Chassidism is *hashgachah pratit* — that everything that happens comes straight from Him, with nothing else interfering, and that all of it is for the best. And one of the first acts in rededicating the Temple was clearing out the foreign gods the Greeks and their sympathisers had put in it.

The haftorah: "And He fixed darkness about Him as booths; gathering of waters, thick clouds of the skies." (II Samuel 22:12) Chassidus is about turning darkness into light, and darkness is the sign of the Greek exile. Chanukah also runs eight days in order to answer the eight days of Sukkot, which the verse's own word for booths calls up.

The quality is bonding of friends, which may be the main principle of the whole movement: that chassidim are one family, and that all Jews are one.

The prophet is Nathan, who shows what it means from both sides. He is the one who reproves King David with a parable, over an act that was the opposite of bonding with a friend. And it is by that same bonding that he later acts for Batsheva against Adonyah's attempt on the throne: Adonyah had gathered his own friends and allies to declare himself king, so David sends Nathan to join Benaiah ben Yehoyada and Zadok the Kohen and the rest, and declare Solomon.

The Levitical city is Anathoth, which may be the darkest of them. The Zohar says Anathoth means poverty. It was a town of Kohanim gone wrong, whose people wanted to kill the prophet Jeremiah, who came from there, and whose prospects in his prophecy are grim. Chassidus came to teach that even Anathoth can be raised. The name comes from *Anat*, a Canaanite warrior-goddess who looks related to Athena — which suits a month about fighting Greece both ways. And the name has a better side: it is the name of the parent of Shamgar, one of Israel's judges and deliverers, and of a man of the tribe of Benjamin, and of one who signed a covenant in the Book of Nehemiah.

The lesson follows last week's. One of the first things Pirkei Avot says is: make for yourself a rabbi, acquire for yourself a friend, and judge every person favourably — acquiring the friend comes straight after making the teacher. The Talmud puts it harder: either companionship or death. Studying with a partner brings clarity, and lets you see how far you have actually got.

## Day 80 · Book 3 · Week 12

"Who is like You among the powerful, O Lord? Who is like You, exalted in the holy place?" *Mi Chamocha Ba'Elim Hashem* — the acronym that gives the Maccabees their name. And the question comes twice, which answers to the two lights the Alter Rebbe brought: the revealed, in law, and the hidden, in Chassidus. The first half of the verse is His greatness out in the open — *elim* meaning the forces of nature — and the second is the hidden kind.

The haftorah is about giving yourself up willingly, and then: "The riders of white donkeys." Which points at Mashiach, who comes on a donkey. The Alter Rebbe's Tanya belongs to this day, and R. Zusya of Anipoli is said to have foretold: with the Tanya the Jewish people will go out to greet the righteous Mashiach.

Sotah's twelfth daf is Miriam and Moshe. The Talmud says Moshe was also called Tuviah, for the great light revealed about him at his birth — which goes with a man named Shneur for the two lights he brought. And Miriam spoke to her father and so persuaded him, and through him the whole people, not to divorce their wives. The Alter Rebbe's daughter Devorah Leah had a conversation of that kind with her father too, about giving up her own life to add years to his, so that the work could go on.

The generation is Arpachshad, Shem's son, whose name breaks into three words: *ohr*, light; *pach*, a vial; and *shad*, from Sh-dai, one of His names, read as *Shomer Daltot Yisrael*, Guardian of the doors of Israel — the Name that goes on a mezuzah. All three are this festival. Light; the small vial of pure oil that lasted eight days instead of one; and the lamp that is lit beside the mezuzah. A mezuzah's first fixing is called *Chanukath HaBayit*, the dedication of a house. The Midrash also reads Sh-dai as the One who told the world *dai*, enough — who set limits on what He made, being Himself without any. Which is this festival's message from the other direction: He can also do what nature does not allow.

The journey arrives at the mountain: "They journeyed from Rephidim and camped in the Sinai desert." Last week's weakness for want of water turns out to have been for the sake of a larger knowledge — as this week gives the Torah of Chassidus.

Arpachshad is also tied to Ur Chasdim, where Abraham was thrown into the furnace for defying Nimrod. Which brings to mind Rabbi Elimelech of Lizhensk's practice: in any idle moment, lying awake, picture a great fire burning in front of you reaching to the heavens, and for the sake of His holiness go against your nature and throw yourself in. The Holy One joins good intentions to great deeds — so you are no longer idle but keeping a commandment.

## Day 81 · Book 4 · Week 12

"And it came to pass, as she prayed long before the Lord, that Eli watched her mouth." She prays at length, and the Kohen Gadol guards her mouth.

Which is the story of this week. Yud Tes Kislev begins with a heavenly decree against the Alter Rebbe for revealing the secrets of Chassidut, and it came out as fifty-three days in prison on invented charges of treason against the Czar. During those days he was visited by the Baal Shem Tov and by the Maggid of Mezritch — whose yahrzeit is Yud Tes Kislev — and told that he was not only absolved of the decree but should teach and reveal more. With his masters having watched his mouth, his teaching afterwards became far more expansive. Chassidim speak of before Petersburg and after Petersburg, Petersburg being where he was held.

The week's quality is that the Torah makes him fit to be *chassid*, pious — and Yud Tes Kislev is the Rosh Hashanah of Chassidut. Hannah in that verse is piety itself.

Proverbs' twelfth chapter keeps contrasting the righteous with the wicked. The difference between a tzadik, last week's word, and a chassid is that the chassid goes past the letter of the law to please the Creator. The chapter is full of that kind of thing: "A good man will obtain favor of the Lord… A righteous man has regard for the desire of his beast, but the mercy of the wicked is cruel… The righteous is more generous than his neighbor… In the road of charity is life, and on the way of its path there is no death."

Two yahrzeits here, both close to the Alter Rebbe. The eighteenth of Kislev is Rabbi Baruch of Mezhibuz, the Baal Shem Tov's grandson, a disciple of the Maggid and one of the leading rebbes of the Alter Rebbe's own day — and it was to him that the Alter Rebbe wrote, once freed, describing the miracles. He once challenged the Alter Rebbe, who is said to have answered: "You may be his grandson in a physical sense; I am his grandson in a spiritual sense."

And the nineteenth is the Maggid of Mezritch himself, the Baal Shem Tov's successor, whose disciples became the leaders of the movement — Rebbe Elimelech of Lizhensk and his brother Rav Zusia of Anipoli, Rebbe Menachem Mendel of Vitebsk, Rebbe Levi Yitzchak of Berditchev, Rebbe Aharon of Karlin, Rebbe Shmuel Shmelke of Nikolsburg and his brother Rebbe Pinchas Horowitz of Frankfurt. On his deathbed he turned to the Alter Rebbe: "Zalmanyu, give me your hand. You will remain alone, you are for yourself — you have your own way. You will need a lot of help from Heaven. I will yearn for you very much, and G-d willing, I will save you from all your troubles." And, by the Chabad tradition, he said: "This day is our Yom Tov."

## Day 82 · Book 5 · Week 12

"Until the sun spreads, and the shadows flee, go around; liken yourself, my beloved, to a gazelle or to a fawn of the hinds, on distant mountains." Then a new chapter: "On my bed at night, I sought him whom my soul loves; I sought him but I did not find him. I will arise now and go about the city, in the market places and in the city squares. I will seek him whom my soul loves; I sought him, but I did not find him."

All of it is ours. The sun spreading is what happened after the release: Chassidut went out across the land — to distant mountains, to a bed at night, which is exile, and into the city, the market, the squares. There was nowhere it could not get into. And taken together the verses are a long ache for Him. The Alter Rebbe's longing for Hashem was such that it gave him a breathing complaint.

Of the seventy souls the twelfth is Saul the son of the Canaanitess. Rashi explains that this is Dinah's son — she had been raped and taken by a Canaanite who meant to marry her. One of the subjects of this month is intermarriage and assimilation, and in Dinah's story Shimon and Levi act with the zeal of Maccabees to get her back. Though their violence and their deceit, unlike the Maccabees', were not right, and Jacob condemned them for it. The tribe of Levi learned in the end what to do with its zeal.

Shevuot's twelfth daf is about buying what is dedicated on condition of using the remainder, and what to do with animals left over from offerings, and then the goat sent to Azazel, which atoned for whatever the inner goat did not. Which has something of Chassidut's own innovation in it: that everything is holy and has a use, that every Jew is holy, and that even the animal soul can be made holy.

Jeremiah's twelfth chapter lays out one disaster after another and rebukes the people — and all the way through calls them "My inheritance" and "My soul's beloved." The Greeks wanted that exact belief gone, that this people had a portion in Him, and the love between them was the thing they could not grasp. "I have abandoned My House, I have forsaken My inheritance; I have delivered My soul's beloved into the hand of her enemies." And later: "after I uproot them, I will return and have pity on them, and I will restore them, each one to his inheritance and each one to his land."

## Day 83 · Book 6 · Week 12

The fourth week of Kislev in psalms: the thirty-fourth, thirty-fifth and thirty-sixth, with the opening of Psalm 42 and a verse of Psalm 89.

Psalm 34 is headed from when David feigned insanity before Avimelech and was driven away. It is mostly instruction, and generous with it. "I bless the Lord at all times; His praise is always in my mouth… I sought the Lord and He answered me; He delivered me from all my fears. ## Day 85 · Book 1 · Week 13

Week thirteen brings in Chanukah, and the starling sings about being seen: "Their seed shall be known among the nations, and their offspring among the peoples; all who see them shall acknowledge them, that they are the seed that G-d has blessed." (Isaiah 61:9)

Which is this week's own mitzvah — *pirsumei nissa*, publicising the miracle, so that everyone can see what was given to this people in the days of the Greeks.

The song's attention to seed points at the kohanim, whose line, unlike nearly everything else in Judaism, runs by the physical male seed; there are even tests for a kohen gene, to tell with near certainty whether a man descends from Aaron. The Maccabees were kohanim, and what they did made the seed of Aaron known among the nations.

And Chanukah comes from *chinuch*. The starling also says that each of us was a seed, planted and tended and blessed by parents and teachers and above all by G-d — and the same or better has to be done for our children and students. Darkness and assimilation are beaten by education in the end.

Pirkei Avot gives Akavia the son of Mahalalel, who is blunt: "Reflect upon three things and you will not come to the hands of transgression. Know from where you came, where you are going, and before whom you are destined to give a judgment and accounting. From where you came — from a putrid drop; where you are going — to a place of dust, maggots and worms; and before whom you are destined to give a judgment and accounting — before the supreme King of Kings, the Holy One, blessed be He."

That is seed again, put much less kindly — and it is pointed straight at what this week defeated. Hellenistic culture valued man, and the human body in particular, above everything. Akavia says the body comes from a putrid drop and ends being eaten by worms, and is then judged by G-d Himself. Keep your eye on G-d, not on man.

Which also shows how much mercy there is in it. Low where we came from and low where we are going, and still in a direct relationship with the King of kings, the way children are with a father. There is a spark of Him in us, and when He punishes it is for our good. Chassidism teaches that we have no idea how precious the body is to Him — precious like the seed in the starling's song.

The pairing is Yesod shebeGevurah, and it could hardly fit better: yesod is foundation, and this week a people held to its foundation, with courage, against a policy of forced assimilation.

## Day 86 · Book 2 · Week 13

"He made them ride upon the high places of the earth, that they would eat the produce of the field. He let them suck honey from a rock, and oil from the mighty part of the crag." (Deuteronomy 32:13) High places, something impossible, and oil — the whole story is in one verse. The word for high places, *bamah*, also means altar. And the oil can be read as the *pintele yid*, the innermost part of a Jew that stays pure whatever happens and shows itself when things get hard.

The haftorah: "From the brightness before Him flamed forth coals of fire." (II Samuel 22:13) Brightness and fire, which is the Menorah — and coals again, which can be burning inside and need one more step before the fire shows.

The quality is sharp discussion with students, *pilpul*. One of the things this people got out of the Greek exile was sharpness and logic. The Greek king's name, *Talmai*, has the same numerical value as *Talmud*, and the Talmud grew greatly out of that encounter — it even carries Greek words.

The prophets are David and Avigail. David is monarchy, and the Maccabees made a dynasty; he is also the proof that a person can be entirely holy and entirely involved in the world at once — Torah and prayer alongside running a government and fighting wars. Chanukah has both halves too: the military victory, and the victory of Jewish wisdom over Greek wisdom, which are David and Solomon respectively. And Avigail kept blood from being spilt by going to David and talking to him about her husband, as the Hasmoneans kept further blood from being spilt by making an alliance with Rome. Both of them had the sharpness this week is about. David's mind was such that the halachah always followed his opinion, and it was Avigail's argument that stopped him.

The Levitical city is 'Almon, which means hidden — from *He'elem*, and from *olam*. Revealing what is hidden is the point of Kislev. Through this miracle you can see that the whole *olam* is a kind of screen and that His power is the real thing. And on Chanukah even the most hidden Jews come out. There are limits on what may be used for Shabbat candles and no such limits for these; every Jew is lit up by it.

## Day 87 · Book 3 · Week 13

"Too awesome for praises, performing wonders! You inclined Your right hand; the earth swallowed them up." Praise for wonders, which is the week's whole work. The haftorah asks for it to be told: "those that sit in judgment, and those that walk on the path, tell of it" — and says the persecution has stopped "between the places of drawing water," which stands for the study of Torah the Greeks were attacking.

Sotah's thirteenth daf is the burial of Jacob and of Joseph, and opens with the room filling with light when Moshe was born. On Jacob's burial, the Talmud notices that the argument with Eisav was not won by reasoning: Eisav was making a baseless claim and demanding proof of this and that, and Chushim son of Dan could not stand to see his grandfather's burial held up, so he went ahead and killed him. Which is almost exactly how the revolt began — Matisyahu, told to slaughter a pig on a pagan altar, refused, and then killed the Jew and the Syrian official who had demanded it.

The generation is Shelah, Arpachshad's son — and Shelah is one of the names of Mashiach. It is also the name of Judah's son, whom he did not give to Tamar.

Rabbi Yosef Jacobson draws the two together. Rabbi Isaac Luryah wrote that the judgment begun on Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur is completed some three months later, in the days of Chanukah. Three months after Judah and Tamar, Judah is told what Tamar has supposedly done and the verdict goes against her. And at that moment she sends word: "I am pregnant by the man who is the owner of these articles. Identify, I beg you, these objects. Who is the owner of this seal, cord and staff?"

Read as the Jew speaking to G-d, the timing is exact. At the moment the prosecuting angels have nearly proved the whole experiment a failure, the Jew sends word that the report is false — if he has strayed here and there it is a surface thing, and underneath he belongs to no one else. And he points at the objects: the cord, which is the wick soaked in oil each night, commemorating a sealed cruse found after the Temple was plundered; the seal on that cruse; and the staff, which he has had to pick up over and over for two thousand years, leaving house and security to find somewhere he could still live as a Jew.

Then Judah answers: "She is right; it is from me that she conceived. She did it because I did not give her to my son Shelah." And Shelah is the name of Mashiach. Read that way, it is G-d saying the fault is His: how can I expect a Jew never to sin, never to try his luck with a world that looks so appealing, when I held back the light of Mashiach so long? "It is I who is guilty of treason," not the Jew. Tamar is an innocent palm tree with one heart to her Father in heaven.

The journey is a hard place: "They journeyed from the Sinai desert and camped in Kivroth hataavah" — the graves of craving. Having received the Torah, the work is burying one's desires, through *itkafia* and *itapcha*. The Baal Shem Tov reads the graves of craving as utter self-nullification in cleaving to G-d, where cravings die and are buried with no chance of coming back. Chanukah is, unlike the other festivals, mainly a spiritual one: there is no commandment to make a meal. It is a holiday for praising Him.

## Day 88 · Book 4 · Week 13

"Now Hannah, she spoke in her heart; only her lips moved, but her voice could not be heard; therefore, Eli thought she had been drunken." This is the clearest demonstration that prayer is a spiritual act before it is anything else. No sound has to leave a person for Him to hear the cry. And this festival is the victory of the spiritual over the material.

The week's quality is that the Torah makes him fit to be *yashar*, correct — literally straight: truthful, straightforward. Yashar is tied particularly to keeping the negative commandments, which draw down revelations beyond the created order. In the story Eli wrongly takes Hannah for drunk, when she was praying out of a broken heart — and praying drunk would break a negative commandment. Her conduct is the conduct of a yashar, past reproach, and what it drew down was a child.

Proverbs' thirteenth chapter carries on setting the righteous against the wicked, and speaks directly about light: "The light of the righteous will rejoice, but the candle of the wicked will ebb away. Only with wickedness does one cause quarrels, but there is wisdom with those who take counsel."

Two yahrzeits. On the twenty-fourth of Kislev, Rabbi Chaim Chizkiya Medini, known for the Sdei Chemed, an encyclopaedic work of law in eighteen volumes. He was born in Jerusalem and lived in Turkey and Crimea before returning; he was considered for Rishon L'Zion, and moved to Hebron to give himself to his studies instead, eventually becoming its Chief Rabbi. Jews and Arabs alike held him a holy man.

And on the twenty-seventh, Rabbi Chaim Tirar of Chernowitz, known for the Be'er Mayim Chayim, his commentary on the Torah, and for Sidduroh Shel Shabat on the holiness of the Sabbath. He was one of the most important disciples of the Maggid of Mezritch, and studied under Rabbi Yechiel Michel of Zlotchov.

## Day 89 · Book 5 · Week 13

"The watchmen who patrol the city found me: 'Have you seen him whom my soul loves?' I had just passed them by, when I found him whom my soul loves; I held him and would not let him go, until I brought him into my mother's house and into the chamber of her who had conceived me. After all the searching and not finding, and after asking the watchmen — Rashi reads them as Moshe and Aharon, and perhaps they are the leaders and prophets and priests of any generation — He is found. That is Chanukah: He shows Himself, in an impossible victory and in oil that burned eight days.

And once found, not let go until He is brought into the mother's house, into the chamber of the one who conceived — which is the rededication of the Temple, the home of the Shechinah.

The third verse is the same one that closed week eight. Standing here instead, after the finding, the hard tone toward the nations reads as a victory shout rather than a refusal.

Of the seventy souls the thirteenth is Levi, and the fit needs no explaining: every kohen and every zealous Maccabee came from him.

Shevuot's thirteenth daf asks whether Yom Kippur atones for everything, repentance or not, and finds three exceptions: one who denies the basis of the Torah, one who ridicules it, and one who annuls circumcision. Denying the Torah's origin and undoing circumcision were two of the sharpest points of conflict with Hellenist culture. The daf also treats the atonement of the kohanim separately from everyone else's.

Jeremiah's thirteenth chapter is the girdle he is told to wear, which rots and becomes useless — and then: "just as the girdle clings to a man's loins, so have I caused the entire house of Israel and the entire house of Judah to cling to Me." The chapter returns to skirts uncovered and pulled over the face, and to darkness: "Give the Lord your God honor before it becomes dark, and before your feet stumble on the dark mountains." Then: "Will a Cushite change his skin, or a leopard his spots?" The leopard, in Jewish tradition, is particularly Greece — for its beauty, its speed, and above all its boldness. The word for its spots also means stains, *kesem*, of the kind tied to impurity. And leopards are known to mate outside their species. The chapter's last line asks when Jerusalem will ever be pure. In the days of the Maccabees, it was.

## Day 90 · Book 6 · Week 13

The fifth week of Kislev in psalms: the thirty-seventh, thirty-eighth and thirty-ninth, with three verses of Psalm 42 and one of Psalm 89.

## Day 92 · Book 1 · Week 14

Week fourteen carries on Chanukah and brings in Rosh Chodesh Teveth, and the domestic goose asks for everything at once: "Give thanks to G-d and call upon His name, make His works known amongst the peoples, sing to Him, make music to Him, speak of all His wonders!" (Psalms 105:1-2) Which is last week's mitzvah again — telling the miracle — now sung.

Teveth is a hard month. It holds the fast of the tenth, when Jerusalem was besieged. Its tribe is Dan, whose mark is strength and the power to multiply. Dan had only one child and looked likely to die out, and became one of the largest tribes. Samson came from Dan, and he too is physical strength and that same power.

This week also holds Didan Netzach, "victory is ours," on the fifth of Teveth — the day of the victory of the books, when the Lubavitcher Rebbe's case over the Lubavitch library was won, and a secular court openly recognised what there is between a Rebbe and his chassidim. It sits well beside the Maccabees' victory, and beside a month that is about valuing what is written.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Chanina, deputy high priest: "Pray for the integrity of the government; for were it not for the fear of its authority, a man would swallow his neighbor alive." Taken plainly and taken as a figure, both: without something above them, the strong eat the weak, in body and in money.

It is a striking place for the deputy Kohen Gadol's teaching to fall — the week a people celebrates being freed from a foreign power by a handful of kohanim, and thanks G-d for delivering the strong into the hands of the weak.

The pairing is Malchut shebeGevurah, and it closes another seven. The Maccabees were hard and disciplined, which is gevurah, and when they had won they founded a line of kings, which is malchut. Like them, we are to act inside the material world, with discipline — which is Teveth's own quality.

The goose's lesson: notice the miracles that happen around you daily, and say them out loud. Recalling them and telling them is one of the better ways to be grateful, and gratitude is a source of blessing and of happiness.

## Day 93 · Book 2 · Week 14

"The cream of cattle and the milk of sheep, with the fat of lambs and rams of Bashan and he goats, with kidneys of wheat, and it would drink the blood of grapes which was as the finest wine." (Deuteronomy 32:14) Cattle, sheep, lambs, rams, goats, fat, wheat, wine — every one of them went into the Temple's offerings, in the week the Temple was dedicated again.

The haftorah: "The Lord thundered from heaven; and the Most High gave forth His voice." (II Samuel 22:14) Thunder is light breaking through darkness, and a miracle is His voice in the open.

The quality is calmness, *yishuv* — which also means to settle, to sit, to dwell. All of which this festival is. The Temple is where He dwells on earth; the war with the Greeks was about being able to serve Him quietly in our own land. And *Chanukah* can be read *chanu k"h*, they rested on the twenty-fifth.

The prophet is King Solomon, the wisest of men — and this festival is about wisdom, Jewish chochmah set against Greek. He built and dedicated the first Temple, and his reign was the prosperity Haazinu's verse describes. He is also calmness itself: *shalom* is the root of his name, and the Tanach shows him settling disputes.

The Levitical city is Elteke, of which little is known beyond its being given to Dan. It is known for a battle in which Sancherib beat the Egyptians, after which he laid siege to Jerusalem. When all looked lost Isaiah told the king not to fear, and the next day Sancherib's army was struck by plague. That happened on Passover, with Israel and the angels saying Hallel that night — which is what we do on this festival. It is said that G-d wanted to make Chezekiah the Mashiach and did not, because he failed to say Hallel after the miracle.

The name also resembles Tekoa, both holding the uncommon pair *tav* and *kuf*. Tekoa was known for its olive oil — the only oil chosen for the Temple — and at the rededication, pure oil had to be brought from there. It took eight days to produce, which is how long the Kohen Gadol's small flask lasted, and how many days this festival runs. Oil also stands for chochmah, the highest part of the intellect, from which inspired thought comes. The Talmud notes that in Tekoa, where olive oil was common, chochmah was common too. And as the Psalm says, the beginning of chochmah is the fear of G-d.

## Day 94 · Book 3 · Week 14

## Day 95 · Book 4 · Week 14

"And Eli said to her: Until when will you be drunk? Throw off your wine from upon yourself." The Kohen Gadol telling her to put the wine down. Which is worth sitting with, because this whole festival began with a Kohen Gadol — Matisiyahu — killing a man who wanted to bring a pagan offering in the Temple. Chanukah means dedication, and it is the Temple dedicated again once the idolatry was cleared out. Serving while drunk is also what killed Aaron's sons at the first dedication of the Tabernacle, which was finished on the twenty-fifth of Kislev.

But notice that Eli says *your* wine. What is wine? Rabbi Meir held that the fruit of the Tree of Knowledge was the grape, and so do the Zohar and a Midrash. The Tree of Knowledge stands for secular wisdom, as the Tree of Life stands for Torah. Rabbi Riskin points out that the Menorah is shaped like a tree, and may be the two trees perfectly combined — and that the kohanim's job is to clean and purify its lights.

So this festival is the fight between a culture tied to the Torah and one tied to secular wisdom alone — but the Torah is not against secular wisdom. It has its place, so long as it dwells in the tents of Shem. Adam's sin was not tasting the Tree of Knowledge; it was tasting it before the Tree of Life. Had he given himself to the Tree of Life first, the other would have become permitted in time.

Which may be why Eli says *your* wine. His job is to keep improper influences out of the Temple, including a person too drunk to judge how much of that tree to take in. Noah had the same trouble — the Zohar says he planted a vineyard to repair Adam's sin, and erred, and got drunk. But Eli does not rule the thing out altogether. There is a place for it, and it may still belong to Channah the tzadeket, in the right dose. That is this festival's message too.

The week's quality is that the Torah makes him *ne'eman*, faithful — from *emunah*. Emunah is what separated the Jews from the Greeks, and emunah is above reason, so a person acting out of it can look irrational to others, or even drunk, exactly as in this story. Part of having it is not being thrown by how it looks from outside.

Proverbs' fourteenth chapter runs on the same contrasts: "The wisest of women — each one built her house, but a foolish one tears it down with her hands… A faithful witness does not lie, but he who speaks lies is a false witness."

The yahrzeits are Ishbitz and Peshischa. On the fourth of Teveth, Rabbi Gershon Chanoch Henoch Leiner of Radzin, the Baal HaTecheles, grandson of the founder; and often this week the founder himself, Rav Mordecai Yosef Leiner of Ishbitz, the Mei Shiloach — a childhood friend and colleague of the Kotzker Rebbe, both of them students of Reb Simcha Bunim of Peshischa. He followed Kotzk and taught there, and in the end the two parted and Ishbitz was founded. His own students included Rav Tzadok HaKohen of Lublin.

## Day 99 · Book 1 · Week 15

What the goose knows is that Torah study matters and that its food comes from Him and not from any person. If we do our part, He will do His.

## Day 101 · Book 3 · Week 15

"People heard, they trembled; a shudder seized the inhabitants of Philistia." Years later it would be this people trembling, when Babylon came and laid its siege.

The haftorah: "Then the people of the Lord went down to the cities. Praise! Praise! Deborah." The word for cities, *shearim*, also means gates — and on the tenth of Teveth Nebuchadnezar came down on the city and closed round its gates. As for "Praise, praise Devorah," Rashi explains that it is said because the spirit of G-d had left her, since she had praised herself. Which belongs to this week too: the Temple lost its spirit because the people did not bless the Torah they studied. All Torah and all prophecy come from Him, and there is no room to praise yourself for it.

Sotah's fifteenth daf sets the Sotah's offering beside the metzora's, and notes that the metzora's purification is the more severe. The metzora's sin is *lashon hara* — which is weighed against all three of the great sins, and is another reason given for the Temple's fall. And he is sent out of the camp, and can only come back once he is clean, which is the exile that followed.

The generation is Peleg, named so because in his days the tower was built and the world's people were split and scattered into different lands. That is the same exile, and the same sentence as the metzora's, who was sent out for having split people apart with his mouth. The Babylonians made a point of taking most of the people out of the land and settling others in parts of it, and the division between Israel and the Babylonian diaspora held for centuries.

And the Talmud teaches that whoever does not show gratitude to another person will end up not showing it to Hashem either.

## Day 106 · Book 1 · Week 16

Week sixteen, and the ducks — plural, which matters — say: "Trust in G-d forever and ever, for G-d, the Eternal, is the strength of worlds." (Isaiah 26:4)

The Talmud calls Teveth the coldest month of the year, "when the body takes pleasure in the body." And Perek Shirah has ducks here rather than a duck. They breed fast and keep large families; they travel in groups and depend on each other to get through the cold.

Which is this month's own story. After the first Temple fell, very few were left — the Book of Jeremiah counts four thousand six hundred exiled to Babylon altogether. And in a fairly short time that community in Babylon became numerous, influential and rich.

The song is about His strength and also about His giving us the power to bring life. *Tzur* means strength and also Creator, or Craftsman; *Yotzer*, from the same root, is used of the One who "fashioned you from the womb." And the verse mixes the Names: the feminine one of *yud* and *hey*, the masculine *Tzur*, and between them the Name that holds both.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai: three who eat at one table and speak no words of Torah have eaten of idolatrous sacrifices — "all tables are filled with vomit and filth, devoid of the Omnipresent." But three who eat at one table and do speak words of Torah have eaten at G-d's own table: "This is the table that is before G-d."

That belongs here because the Temple fell over how little Torah study was thought to be worth. And Rabbi Shimon, who knew the hidden part of the Torah better than anyone, is teaching how to serve Him with the body — which is the deeper reading of the body taking pleasure in the body. The Lubavitcher Rebbe explains that serving Him with the body, not only with mind and soul, reaches further: to His essence. An ordinary meal becomes the table of G-d. The same is true of marriage, which can be the holiest thing a person does or the most profane, depending entirely on the two people and what they intend.

The pairing is Gevurah shebeTiferet: recovering from the loss, and using discipline to get back to the balanced beauty of the Torah.

The ducks' own lesson is to lean on Him completely, the way you would lean on a rock.

## Day 107 · Book 2 · Week 16

## Day 113 · Book 1 · Week 17

Week seventeen, the last of Teveth, and the bee-eater says: "I will whistle to them and gather them, for I have redeemed them, and they shall increase as they have before increased." (Zechariah 10:8) The increase is Dan again. The whistle is a shepherd's.

This week holds the yahrzeit of the Alter Rebbe, on the twenty-fourth of Teveth. He died largely of the flight from Napoleon. He had backed Russia in that war, because he judged that Napoleon's egalitarian promises would end in assimilation — and that spiritual persecution of that kind was more dangerous than the physical oppression of the Russian government, much as the Greeks had been more dangerous than other empires.

On the flight, the Alter Rebbe rode in the third carriage and his grandson Rabbi Nachum in the first, and at every crossroads they asked him which way. At one of them Rabbi Nachum misheard. When the mistake came to light, much later, "the Alter Rebbe sighed deeply and said: 'How good is it when a grandson follows in the path of his grandfather — and the opposite is true when a grandfather has to follow the path in which his grandson leads him.' … The mistake at the crossroads caused all kinds of troublesome detours, and soon after Alter Rebbe passed away in Piena."

The whistle, then, is the ways He uses to turn a lost Jew back. He is the shepherd and we are the flock. The Zohar calls Moses *Rayah Mehemnah*, a faithful shepherd and a shepherd of faith, and the leader of each generation is that generation's Moses. The point of following such advice is not to lose your way in the dark, which is exactly what happened in that story.

Seventeen is the numerical value of *tov*, good — and it is also the number of the seventeenth of Tammuz, which began the Temple's end. The Talmud ties the two kinds of loss together: the passing of tzadikim is likened to the burning of the Temple. The Lubavitcher Rebbe answers the difficulty the same way for both: this is a descent for the sake of an ascent, and the ascent is great enough to make the exile worth it, there being no other way to reach that rung. The passing of a tzadik opens a level nothing else reaches, and that is the only reason for it. So seventeen really is good, with the extent of the good hidden for now. It is also the value of *cheit*, sin, which is the one thing holding the redemption back — because of our sins we were exiled from our land.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Chanina the son of Chachina'i: "One who stays awake at night, or travels alone on the road, and turns his heart to idleness, has forfeited his life." The night is exile. In it you cannot be alone and occupied with nothing; you have to be gathered, listening for the whistle, and studying — or you lose the road.

Rebbe Nachman reads the same words the other way round. Travelling alone is making your own path in prayer and hitbodedut; *batalah*, idleness, he hears as *bitul*, self-nullification; and "forfeits his life," *mitchayev et nafsho*, he reads as making his soul such that the whole world is obliged to exist for it. Which is the condition of a generation's tzadik.

The pairing is Tiferet shebeTiferet. And the bee-eater's Hebrew name, *Rachamah*, has *rachamim* in it — mercy, which is tiferet's other name. Its lesson is to be sure He is with you and guiding you through the bad part, and also to be listening, so that when the call comes you do not miss it.

## Day 114 · Book 2 · Week 17

"They sacrificed to demons, which have no power, deities they did not know, new things that only recently came, which your forefathers did not fear." (Deuteronomy 32:17) Idolatry again, which is Teveth and is Dan.

And again the verse can be turned: would they sacrifice to demons? No. Their power was G-d; they did not know new ones. Which is worth stopping on, because the verse names what makes assimilation attractive. It is *new* — then as now, the Torah gets painted as out of date. And it comes "from close by." What pulls hardest in another culture is the part that most resembles our own. The Greeks and the Hellenized Jews came from close; what they championed, wisdom and humanism, was often very near Jewish ideals.

The haftorah goes with the positive reading: "He sent from on high and He took me; He drew me out of many waters." (II Samuel 22:17) And many waters, as Book 1 has it, is the struggle of making a living.

Which is the quality: *miut schorah*, minimised business activity. Money and the getting of it turn idolatrous very easily — it becomes the end, and we forget we wanted it in order to serve Him better and provide for a family. So business is to be conducted with *miut*, smallness and humility, with study and service still the main thing.

The prophet is Obadiah, who prophesied like Michaia in Ahab's days, when idolatry was everywhere. Ahab is the opposite of this quality: he took another man's field by force and had the owner killed, and for that he was sentenced from Heaven. Obadiah is the quality itself — he used his own money to keep a hundred prophets alive and hidden from Ahab and Jezebel, and that act earned him prophecy. His name says it: servant of G-d. He understood whose work he was actually doing.

His prophecy is that G-d loves Jacob and hates Esau, which is the message in a fight against assimilation. Much of what drew Jews to Roman culture and its offshoots was how like Judaism they looked. Esau is Jacob's brother, after all. And Jacob, meeting him, says: "My master knows that the children are tender, and the flocks and the cattle, which are raising their young, depend upon me, and if they overdrive them one day, all the flocks will die. Now, let my master go ahead before his servant, and I will move at my own slow pace." He did not want Esau near his family — and, reading the flocks as well as the children, he did not want his possessions exposed to Esau's appetite either.

The Levitical city is Gath-rimmon, the winepress of pomegranates: out in the world and in its dark, there is a great deal of good available, and a person can come back as full as that fruit.

## Day 115 · Book 3 · Week 17

"All the inhabitants of Canaan melted. May dread and fright fall upon them." And the haftorah could not suit Dan better: "Then ruled a remnant among the mighty of the nations; the Lord dominated the strong for me." A remnant ruling over the mighty, which is being small and then not.

Sotah's seventeenth daf is the writing of the curses on the scroll that gets dissolved in the water, and how curses can be inferred from blessings — and how even in the words for man and woman there is *fire* waiting, if the Divine Presence does not dwell between the couple. Both halves belong to this month: blessings turned to curses, and two Temples left by the Presence and then burned.

The daf also tells how Avraham was rewarded for refusing the spoils of the king of Sodom. Which runs alongside the Alter Rebbe. Napoleon was offering Jews more money and more freedom, and he saw where it led. The king of Sodom had said, take the goods and I will take the souls — and Avraham would not. And where Avraham refused even the smallest personal thing, Napoleon went looking for the smallest personal thing belonging to the Alter Rebbe, who had set fire to his own house before leaving, so that nothing was found.

The generation is Serug, Reu's son, and his name is a further gathering: from *lesareg*, to interlace, to interweave. More than rejoining a flock — being woven in. Which is the Alter Rebbe's own innovation: that chassidim are one family, bound unbreakably. The Hayom Yom for the twenty-fourth of Teveth has it exactly. Asked what the Alter Rebbe meant by the "ways of Chassidus" and by "Chassidus," the Tzemach Tzedek answered: the ways of Chassidus are that all chassidim are to be like one family, with affection, as the Torah teaches. And Chassidus is vitality — to bring life and light into everything, to shed light even on what is undesirable, "to become aware of one's own evil exactly as it is, in order to correct it."

The journey is to bricks: "They journeyed from Rimmon Perez and camped in Libnah." Libnah is from *leveinim*, bricks, which belong to the labour in Egypt — where at the end they were not even given straw. In Torah Or, quoting the Zohar, the Alter Rebbe explains that that labour has a counterpart in study: *leveinim*, bricks, points at *libun hilchesa*, the clarifying of law, and *chomer*, mortar, at *kal vachomer*, one of the main rules of interpretation. The hard labour of exile can be exchanged for the labour of study. Last week was being fruitful; this week is studying properly — which is the thing this month remembers the lack of.

## Day 116 · Book 4 · Week 17

"And Eli answered and said: Go in peace, and the God of Israel will grant your request which you have asked of Him."

The Hebrew is *Lechi L'Shalom*, which is *go to peace* rather than *go in peace* — and *Lech B'Shalom*, go in peace, is said only of those who have died. ## Day 120 · Book 1 · Week 18

Week eighteen is Rosh Chodesh Shevat, and the grasshopper asks a question: "I lift my eyes up to the mountains, where shall my help come from?" (Psalms 121:1)

It is answered ten weeks back. The swift, the eighth creature, sings the verse that comes next: "My help comes from the Lord, Creator of Heaven and Earth." Eighteen and eight, question and answer — and eight is the number of what is beyond nature. The grasshopper's song tends to land in the weeks of Vaera or Bo, which are the plagues, locusts among them: help arriving from Him with nature set aside entirely.

Shevat is Tu B'Shvat, the New Year of the Trees, kept on the fifteenth — though the Mishnah has a dispute about whether it should be the first. Which is itself the month's subject: we keep the trees' new year in the middle of winter, and that is faith.

The tribe is Asher, and the word is *ta'anug*, pleasure or delight; the Sixth Lubavitcher Rebbe notes that *asher* itself means delight, from *ashruni*. Jacob blesses Asher that he will bring delicacies to the king. On Tu B'Shvat we drink wine and eat many kinds of fruit — and the delights of this month are intellectual too.

Shevat belongs to the Oral Torah. It was on Rosh Chodesh that Moses began going back over everything he had taught in forty years, which is the whole Book of Deuteronomy. The Chidushei HaRim says that every new Torah insight a person has comes to him in Shevat.

And passing on the Oral Torah needs one trait above all: humility. Without it a person cannot teach purely and plainly what he received from the one before him. Humility is Moses' own mark, the humblest of men and the first to hand it on. Which may be why in Perek Shirah the insects — the humblest creatures — are the ones singing through all four weeks of Shevat. As David said, "I am a worm and not a man."

Eighteen is *chai*, life, which is why gifts are given in multiples of it. Rosh Chodesh Shevat and Tu B'Shvat are a celebration of life in general, not only human life. And *chai* is also a thing's essence: the Ba'al Shem Tov and the Alter Rebbe were both born on Chai Elul, the eighteenth, the life and essence of Elul; Rebbe Nachman's yahrzeit is the eighteenth of Tishrei; Lag Ba'Omer is the eighteenth of Iyar. Eighteen is also the number of blessings in the Amidah, which is called simply *Tefilah* because it is prayer's essence — and prayer is the recognition that a Jew's life is not natural at all.

Pirkei Avot gives Rabbi Nechunia the son of Hakanah: "One who accepts upon himself the yoke of Torah is exempted from the yoke of government duties and the yoke of worldly cares; but one who casts off the yoke of Torah is saddled with" both. Take up the Tree of Life, which is above the world, or be handed the world's obligations instead. Calling the Torah a yoke also says what acquiring it costs. Rabbi Mendel of Kotsk points out that plenty of sages had worldly work and even professions, and did not experience it as a yoke or a worry.

The pairing is Netzach shebeTiferet. A tree is a balance of root, trunk, branch and leaf, and only the balance keeps it up: no roots and it falls, too many branches and it falls, too few leaves and it cannot make enough to grow. In Shevat, mid-winter, it has to hold on. The same balance is wanted between the two yokes, and what it is differs from person to person and from one part of a life to another.

The grasshopper's lesson is that help comes from Him, provided we are willing to look up past our own small view. The Midrash reads the mountains as the patriarchs, and says it is largely for their deeds that we are saved — so look past the present situation, at the whole of what you are and where you came from.

## Day 121 · Book 2 · Week 18

"You forgot the Mighty Rock Who bore you; you forgot the G-d Who delivered you." (Deuteronomy 32:18) Shevat is a month of nature, and the verse puts His relation to us as birth, which is the most natural figure there is. But Shevat is also what stands above nature, and the Oral Torah, and *ta'anug*, and *emunah* — keeping the trees' new year in the depths of winter.

And the verse can be read as a prayer instead: Rock, they are your children; forget completely our sins; G-d delivers you. The haftorah goes with that: "He delivered me from my mighty enemy; from them that hated me; for they were too powerful for me." (II Samuel 22:18) Help against odds, which is nature set aside.

The quality is *miut derech eretz*, minimised worldly activity — which is the same thing Pirkei Avot says this week from the other side. When a person's affairs are conducted small and humbly, His hand becomes much easier to see in them.

The prophet is Achiah HaShiloni, who may represent being above nature better than anyone: Maimonides records that he lived longer than any other prophet, and he taught both Eliyahu and the Ba'al Shem Tov. It was precisely because those two kept their worldly activity small that they merited having him revealed to them. And Achiah told the Ba'al Shem Tov that he had to reveal himself to the world — to be involved in it, but in the way of *miut*, smallness. The Ba'al Shem Tov himself spoke about being a genuinely good person with a simple connection to G-d: both *derech eretz*, being a mentsch, and caring for the *amei ha'aretz*, the simple folk.

The Levitical city is Mishal, which means a prayer, a request. Which is the grasshopper's question, and the eighteen blessings, and this month's faith and delight. Mishal also has the letters of *moshel*, to rule — and in prayer we accept who the Ruler is.

## Day 122 · Book 3 · Week 18

"With the arm of Your greatness may they become as still as a stone, until Your people cross over, O Lord." Still as a stone — and entering Shevat we are reminded of the four levels in nature: *domem*, the silent or mineral; *tzomeach*, the growing; *chai*, the living; *medaber*, the speaking. Each week of this month touches one of them, and this week's verse gives us the stone.

The haftorah begins something that will run through Shevat and Adar: what each tribe did in the fight against Sisera. This week is Ephraim and Benjamin, whom Rashi reads as Joshua and Shaul, and how they fought Amalek. Joshua is the Oral Torah, which is Shevat; Shaul belongs to Purim and Adar. And though Amalek is Adar's business, Amalek is the opposite of *emunah* — *Amalek* has the same numerical value as *safek*, doubt. The war against Sisera was a test of faith that not all the tribes passed, and which tribe showed faith and which showed doubt is the thread from here to Adar.

Sotah's eighteenth daf continues with the writing of the curses, and its theme is that things done twice rather than once, or divided rather than whole, or for two people rather than one, are invalid. Nature is multiplicity — and all of it still comes from Him.

The generation is Nahor, Serug's son and Avraham's grandfather. He lived in Ur Kasdim, where Avraham was born, and he had a grandson also called Nahor — continuity through children, as in nature. The name comes from *nachar*, to blow the nose, to sneeze, to snort. Which connects to the breath blown into Adam's nose, and so to nature and the world's making. And sneezing is a physical pleasure, which is this month's quality. The Talmud says that sneezing during prayer is a good sign, since as one is relieved below, so above.

The journey is to being broken: "They journeyed from Libnah and camped in Rissah." Rissah means broken — and only through that brokenness and smallness can a person actually take the Torah in. Rabbi Jacobson notes that Rissah also means a stopped-up well in Arabic: to draw water from the Torah's well, you have to feel that small. Last week was studying properly; this week is being broken enough to receive what is taught.
