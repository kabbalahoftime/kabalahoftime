# The Song of the Soul

### An introduction to the Kabbalah of Time

---

## The song you do not know the words to

We spend much of our life in spiritual darkness. We go about our days with
great uncertainty, without the benefit of sage advice or guidance, and yet
somehow we keep going — attaching ourselves to values that confuse the mind
and the heart, ignoring the real needs and wants of the soul. We become so
busy with our own affairs, and so distracted by the avalanche of superfluous
information directed at us, that we blind ourselves to the signs all around:
the lessons and the warnings presented to us at every moment.

Certain instances, however, wake us. In those times, which are like lightning
bolts of clarity, we realize there is something greater, something beyond this
physical plane and our worldly concerns.

The reality is that the soul needs to sing.

But what are we to do if we do not know the melody or the lyrics?

The Ba'al Shem Tov, the founder of the Chassidic movement, explains that this
is the feeling behind the shofar blast on Rosh Hashanah. The shofar is the
most basic and primal expression of the soul — a cry without words, because
the words have not been found yet. It is with this cry that the Jewish people
wake at the start of every year.

This book has one objective: to bring us closer to that song.

And it makes one claim about where the song is written down.

**The Jewish calendar itself is its sheet music.**

---

## Live with the times

Rabbi Schneur Zalman of Liadi, the Alter Rebbe, taught that one should "live
with the times." He did not mean keeping up with the news. He meant that every
day of the Jewish year carries its own meaning, its own energy, its own
lesson — and that a person can live inside that meaning rather than beside it.

Most of us experience the calendar as a container. Appointments go in it. It
tells us when things are. The claim here is the opposite: the calendar is not
a container but a teacher, and it has something specific to say on each day of
the year. It only says it once a year. Miss this week's lesson and it comes
back around in three hundred and sixty-four days.

Everything that follows is an attempt to hear what it is saying.

---

## Where the method comes from

The method is not new. It is borrowed from a commandment that Jews have kept
for three thousand years, and then extended.

**The Counting of the Omer** — *Sefirat Ha'Omer* — is a Torah commandment to
count the days from Passover to Shavuot. The omer itself was a measure of
barley brought as an offering in the Temple. Barley, in those days, was
primarily animal food. Hold on to that; it matters later.

The count runs forty-nine days: seven weeks of seven.

What happened across those forty-nine days the first time is the reason the
count exists. In Egypt the Jewish people had sunk to the forty-ninth level of
spiritual impurity. In the forty-nine days after they left, they climbed —
level by level, day by day — until they arrived at Mount Sinai so refined that
the entire nation could camp there in complete harmony: *as one person with
one heart.* Only then could they receive the Torah.

So the forty-nine days are not a waiting period. They are a ladder, and the
nation climbed it one rung a day.

### The grid

Every year, during the count, there is a custom to spend each day on a
different combination of **sefirot** — the Divine attributes, which are also
found inside every person.

The arrangement is simple. There are seven emotional sefirot, and the count
runs seven weeks, so **each week is one sefirah**:

> Chesed · Gevurah · Tiferet · Netzach · Hod · Yesod · Malchut
>
> kindness · discipline · harmony · endurance · acknowledgement · foundation ·
> kingship

And **each day within the week is a sefirah inside that sefirah**. The first
day is *chesed shebechesed*, kindness within kindness. The second is *gevurah
shebechesed*, discipline within kindness. And so on, forty-nine combinations,
each one a specific place to stand for one day.

These combinations are printed in most prayerbooks. None of this is hidden.

## The discovery

Here is the observation the whole system rests on.

**Lag Ba'Omer** is the thirty-third day of the Omer. It is the day the plague
stopped among Rabbi Akiva's twenty-four thousand students, who had died for
their lack of unity and respect for one another — the exact opposite of the
*one person with one heart* at Sinai. It is also the yahrzeit of Rabbi Shimon
Bar Yochai, author of the Zohar, the foundational text of Kabbalah.

Day thirty-three of forty-nine is **two-thirds** of the way through the count.

Now look at the year.

The week in which Lag Ba'Omer falls is the **thirty-third week of the
fifty-two**. Which is also two-thirds of the way through.

> *"Incredibly, just as Lag Ba'Omer takes place on the thirty-third day of the
> omer, two thirds into the counting between Passover and Shavuot, so too — and
> this is quite remarkable — the week of Lag Ba'Omer falls two thirds into the
> Jewish year, exactly on the thirty-third week."*

The forty-nine days and the fifty-two weeks line up.

Which means **each week of the year parallels a day of the Omer**, and carries
that day's combination of sefirot. The ladder that the nation climbed in
forty-nine days is the same ladder the year climbs in fifty-two weeks. It is
therefore possible to do the work of the Omer — the specific, dated,
sefirah-by-sefirah work — across the entire year, not only in the spring.

That is the Kabbalah of Time. Everything else is detail.

## What sings: Perek Shirah

If each week has a place on the ladder, what does it have to say?

**Perek Shirah** — *Chapter of Song* — is an ancient text, printed in only a
handful of prayerbooks, and not well known. Its authorship is uncertain, and
many attribute it to King David. The text hints at this itself: it describes
David's encounter with a frog just after he finished the Book of Psalms, in
which the frog says, *"David! Do not become proud, for I recite more songs and
praises than you."*

Perek Shirah is a work of enormous lyricism. In it the sun sings, and the moon,
and heaven and earth, and the trees, and the animals — each with its own verse
from Scripture. It is like a great orchestra in which, instead of musicians,
every element and living being contributes to one masterpiece: all of creation
exclaiming the greatness of its Creator. Among sacred Jewish texts it is a
pioneer when it comes to the environment.

And here is the thing that makes it usable:

**There are exactly fifty-two animals in Perek Shirah. One for each week of
the year.**

### Why animals

It is well known, in Judaism and elsewhere, that we can learn how to behave by
watching animals.

The Talmud says that had the Torah not been given, we would have learned
modesty from the cat, to avoid theft from the ant, to avoid promiscuity from
the dove, and *derech eretz* — proper conduct — from the rooster. The Book of
Proverbs tells the lazy man to go and watch the ant, which has no supervisor
and still gathers its food in summer. The Book of Job teaches that we learn to
glorify God by watching birds. And in Pirkei Avot, Rabbi Yehuda ben Teima says
to be *bold like the leopard, swift like the eagle, fast like the deer, and
courageous like the lion*, in order to do the will of your Father in Heaven.

There is a reason this works. Human beings are full of paradox and internal
conflict. Animals are not. Their emotional attributes are strong and clear,
without room for human subtleties — so a trait is easier to see in them than in
ourselves.

And notice what the Omer offering was made of. Barley. Animal food. During the
Omer we are working on our emotional characteristics — our animal qualities —
and the offering says so.

The Lubavitcher Rebbe draws out the whole arc through food. On Passover we eat
*matzah*, which is the total nullification of ego. Through the Omer the
offering is animal food, reflecting the struggle to refine our animal
characteristics. And on Shavuot, once the character has been worked, leavened
bread is brought into the Temple for the first time.

## What instructs: Pirkei Avot

Alongside the song there is a teaching.

**Pirkei Avot** — *Chapters of the Fathers* — is the part of the Mishnah in
which the great rabbis each set down, in a few words, what they considered most
essential for living well. It is studied in most communities during the very
weeks of the Omer.

It can also be read as *Father Chapters*: these are the founding principles
from which the rest of the Torah's teachings descend, the way children descend
from parents.

The rabbis of the first four chapters are arranged so that **each rabbi
corresponds to a week of the year.**

So a week now has three things: a place on the sefirot ladder, a creature with
its song, and a rabbi with his teaching.

---

## The seven books

Each of those threads was followed through all fifty-two weeks, and each became
a book. There are seven, and they are the seven days of the week — one book for
each day.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Book 1** | Song of Nature | the week's theme, its creature from Perek Shirah, its teaching from Pirkei Avot |
| **Book 2** | Song of Moses | the forty-eight qualities by which Torah is acquired, a prophet, a Levitical city, the week's verse of Haazinu |
| **Book 3** | Song of the Sea | the line that runs to David — Ruth's genealogy, the haftara, Tractate Sotah, the journeys through the desert |
| **Book 4** | Song of Hannah | Solomon's writings and Hannah's song, the sixth chapter of Pirkei Avot, the week's yahrzeit |
| **Book 5** | Song of Songs | the Song of Songs through the year, the seventy souls, Jeremiah, Tractate Shevuot |
| **Book 6** | General Remedy | the Psalms of the week with Perek Shirah, the ten of the Tikkun HaKlali, Psalm 89 |
| **Book 7** | Gathering of Songs | all the verses of the week's songs from Books 1–6, rearranged into a single song |

Seven books, fifty-two weeks each. **Three hundred and sixty-four readings** —
which is a Jewish year of fifty-two complete weeks.

## The day is the year

One more turn, and it is the one that makes this a daily practice rather than
a weekly one.

The Omer's forty-nine days are a microcosm of the whole year. So the same
structure that divides the year can divide a week, and the same structure that
divides a week can divide a day.

For a daily connection across the whole year, you subdivide each week, giving a
sefirah to each day. A person is then keeping **seven separate Omer counts at
once**. The first day of the year is *chesed shebechesed shebechesed* — the
first day of the first week of the first series of sevens. And Lag Ba'Omer is
not only *hod shebehod* but *hod shebehod shebehod*.

The same thing can be done upward. The fifty-two weeks can be read as
fifty-two years of a life, beginning at birth and starting over at fifty-two.
King Solomon and Shmuel HaNavi both lived exactly fifty-two years. In the
Passover Haggadah, Rabbi Elazar ben Azariah says he is "like a man of seventy"
— and the Vilna Gaon explains that he was eighteen, and knew himself to be a
reincarnation of Shmuel, so he counted eighteen plus fifty-two.

Applied to history, the same pattern runs through the Sabbatical and Jubilee
years.

Down into the day, up into a life, up again into history. The same ladder,
every time.

## The app

All of this used to live in seven books and a blog. It now also lives in an app
that does one thing: **it tells you where you are standing today.**

Open it and the day's position is already worked out — which week of the
fifty-two, which rung of the sefirot, which creature is singing, which rabbi is
teaching, which of the seven books belongs to today. The day's own book is
always waiting on the Binah card.

Each card shows the structure first, then the text that the structure is
measuring, with a link to its source. The structure comes first because the
structure is the point: the texts are chosen to fit the count, not the other
way around. Tap the ⓘ beside any cycle to read what it means.

## How to begin

Not at the beginning.

> **Start with the day you are in.**

This can be read from end to end, but that is not what it is for. It is meant
to be experienced one cycle at a time: connect with the energy of the day you
are actually standing in, absorb what is there, and let it change the conduct
of that day. Tomorrow there will be another.

If you want a rule small enough to keep: **begin with three.** Three practices,
three verses, three minutes. Not everything. The small beginning that actually
begins.

## One more thing you will need

*Emunah* — faith.

When the Sea of Reeds had not yet split, the Jewish people were cornered: the
Egyptian army behind, deep water ahead. They hesitated, and panicked, even
after everything they had just seen. Then Nachshon ben Aminadav walked into the
water. The sea split when it reached his nostrils, and everyone followed.

The Midrash says God wanted the people to act out of emunah, and that Nachshon
is how we learn to conduct our lives. Emunah is something a person develops —
the word is linked to *omanut*, craft, a thing you get better at — but to begin
you need a little of it already. Enough to step in.

The obstacles in Nachshon's way were removed because he was determined to bring
God's will into the world.

That is all that is being asked here, too. Not certainty. Just enough to start
counting.
